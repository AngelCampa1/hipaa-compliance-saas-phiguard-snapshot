Vendor inventory hygiene starts with finding the vendors nobody calls "vendors."

The EHR is obvious.

The less obvious list is where small clinics often find gaps:

Reminder tool.
Fax service.
Answering service.
Cloud backup.
Shared drive.
Billing add-on.
IT support.
Document destruction.
Scheduling widget.
Transcription or AI feature.

A good inventory row should say what the vendor does, whether PHI is involved, BAA status, last review date, next review due, subprocessors if known, and the owner.

The point is not to make the spreadsheet impressive.

The point is to make the next vendor question answerable in under a minute.

Notification previews deserve their own review.

A clinic may train staff not to put PHI in a task title, then let the task tool send rich comment previews to email, lock screens, or broad channels.

That is not a task problem alone.

It is a distribution problem.

Review:

What shows in email alerts?
What shows on mobile lock screens?
Who receives watcher notifications?
Do attachments trigger previews?
Can comments be restricted?
Can sensitive updates require an intentional click into the controlled record?

The minimum necessary idea should shape the push notification, not only the database.

If the preview contains more context than the recipient needs, the workflow needs tightening.

- Review status: approved

- Notes: Grounded in four-factor assessment documentation guidance; avoids breach advice.

Four-factor assessment files often fail in the verbs.

"Assumed."
"Believed."
"Likely."
"Probably."

Those words may be honest, but they need evidence next to them.

For each factor, ask what the clinic actually knows:

What PHI was involved?
Who received or accessed it?
Was there evidence it was viewed?
What containment or mitigation happened?
When did each step happen?

This is not about forcing every incident into the same conclusion.

It is about making the reasoning visible enough that a later reviewer can follow the path from facts to determination.

Weak comfort language is not the same as a documented assessment.

A sanctions log should show consistency, not drama.

Small clinics sometimes avoid documenting minor policy issues because documentation feels too severe.

That creates the opposite problem.

If only extreme events are documented, the clinic loses the middle of the enforcement record:

Coaching.
Retraining.
Written warning.
Access restriction.
Follow-up.
Escalation after repeats.

The log can stay controlled, factual, and limited. It can reference roles and case numbers rather than putting unnecessary personal detail in the primary record.

The practical question is: can the clinic show how it applied its own policy over time?

That is an operations question before it is anything else.

Risk analysis starts to wobble when the asset inventory is stale.

Small clinics add systems quietly:

A scanner.
A shared drive folder.
A new billing portal.
A provider's tablet.
A reminder service.
A telehealth add-on.
A backup tool.

If the inventory misses the asset, the risk analysis can miss the exposure.

The useful habit is simple:

When a system is added, changed, retired, or assigned to a new owner, update the inventory.

Track what ePHI it stores or transmits, who has access, which vendor supports it, and what safeguards apply.

An annual review helps. A change-triggered update keeps the annual review from becoming archaeology.

An access matrix is where "minimum necessary" becomes operational.

Without it, access decisions drift into personality and convenience:

"She helps with everything."
"He may need it someday."
"That role has always had admin."
"It is faster if everyone can see it."

The matrix should map role to system to permitted access.

Front desk.
Billing.
Clinical support.
Provider.
Practice administrator.
Contractor.
Temporary coverage.

Then access reviews can compare actual permissions against expected permissions.

The matrix will not make every decision easy, but it gives the clinic a reference point that is better than memory.

Access control is easier to defend when the role logic is written down before the exception appears.

A BAA renewal is a good moment to ask what changed.

Not just:

"Can you send the updated agreement?"

Ask:

Did the vendor add AI features?
Did subprocessors change?
Did the hosting location change?
Was there a reportable security incident?
Did the legal entity change?
Did the service scope change?
Are the same safeguards still in place?

The renewal decision should have a short assessment summary behind it.

Renew as-is.
Renew with revised terms.
Terminate or replace.

Whatever the answer, put the reasoning in the vendor record.

The renewal file should show that someone reassessed the relationship, not just collected another signature.

Spreadsheets become PHI systems by accident.

They start as a quick tracker.

Then someone adds a patient identifier.
Then someone adds a date.
Then the file moves to a shared drive.
Then permissions widen because another role needs "just temporary" access.

At that point, the clinic has a PHI workflow, whether it named it that or not.

The fix starts with inventory, not blame.

Where is the file?
What PHI categories are inside it?
Who can open it?
Is access still needed?
Is there a safer system for the same work?
What retention rule applies?

What is the spreadsheet your clinic would least want to explain during a review?

Policy review should leave a trail.

Not just "updated."

A useful policy review record says:

Which policy was reviewed.
Which version was active.
What changed.
Why it changed.
Who approved it.
Who needs to acknowledge it.
What training or task updates follow.

This matters because policies connect to daily work.

If the access policy changes but the access review checklist stays old, the clinic has a documentation mismatch.

If the incident reporting policy changes but staff never acknowledge the new escalation path, the update may not reach practice.

Policy review is not just document editing.

It is a workflow that should connect policy, acknowledgement, training, and evidence.

Former staff access is not a theoretical issue.

It is one of the easiest access problems to create because separation is busy:

Patients still need coverage.
Schedules need updating.
Payroll needs closing.
Devices need collecting.
Managers assume IT handled it.
IT assumes the manager sent the list.

The clean process assigns one owner to verify removal across every PHI-touching system.

The evidence should include the system, account, removal date, person who removed it, and person who verified it.

Do not rely on "we disabled the main login" if the person also had vendor portal, shared inbox, cloud drive, or mobile access.

Offboarding is a checklist because memory is not enough.

## Post 13 - 15:51 CST
- Pillar: Incident response
- CTA type: resource_link
- Review status: approved

- Notes: Grounded in triage worksheet and evidence log; avoids legal conclusion.

The evidence log is the backbone of incident response.

It should capture each action in order:

Report received.
Initial intake completed.
Containment started.
Access removed or credentials changed.
Relevant logs preserved.
Four-factor assessment completed.
Decision documented.
Notifications or follow-up handled if required.
Incident closed.

The exact path depends on the facts. The habit should not.

If the clinic waits until the end to summarize everything, the record will miss timing, uncertainty, and the actual sequence of decisions.

A good evidence log does not need dramatic language.

It needs dates, actions, owners, and notes that another reviewer can follow.
