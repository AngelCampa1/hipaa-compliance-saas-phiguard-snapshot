  HIPAA setup does not start when the waiting room is full.

  It starts when the practice becomes a covered entity and begins creating, receiving, maintaining, or transmitting PHI.

  For many new clinics, the operating risk arrives earlier than the first normal clinic day. The covered entity trigger may be tied to electronic claims, eligibility checks, or a clearinghouse receiving information on the practice's behalf. The PHI-handling risk may also start when the EHR is configured with real patient data.

  The mistake is treating HIPAA as a "month three" administrative project. By then, the clinic may already have vendors touching PHI, staff using shared setup credentials, training delivered verbally with no record, and no written answer to the most basic question: why does HIPAA apply to us?

  A better pre-opening file starts with one page:

  Covered entity determination
  Privacy Officer designation
  Security Officer designation
  Initial PHI system inventory
  Vendor list with BAA status
  Notice of Privacy Practices draft
  First risk analysis scope

  The point is operating discipline: a new clinic should know what it is before it decides how to run.

  Operational takeaway: document covered entity status before patient data starts moving. That written determination becomes the first page of the compliance program, not a cleanup memo after launch.

  For new practices, what is usually missed first: covered entity status, BAAs, training records, or risk analysis?

post: |
  The first vendor mistake in a new clinic is not choosing the wrong EHR.

  It is letting vendors touch PHI before the BAA register exists.

  New practices often sign up for tools in a rush: EHR, practice management, billing, clearinghouse, phone system, fax, cloud backup, email, IT support, shredding, answering service. Each relationship feels like setup work. But several of those vendors may create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI on the clinic's behalf.

  A BAA is not an after-opening paperwork item. It belongs before access.

  The startup checklist should have a vendor table with practical fields:

  Vendor
  Service
  PHI involved
  Business associate? yes/no/unknown
  BAA signed date
  Access start date
  Evidence location
  Renewal or review date
  Internal owner

  The most useful word in that table is "unknown." It tells the Privacy Officer where to stop onboarding until the question is answered.

  Do not rely on "we use a HIPAA-friendly vendor" as proof. Confirm the BAA is executed, retain the copy, and connect it to the system or service that will handle PHI.

  Operational takeaway: create the BAA register before the vendor stack is live. Then make "BAA status checked" a required step before any new vendor receives PHI.

post: |
  The cleanest time to fix access control is before anybody has bad habits.

  A new clinic has a rare advantage: no years of access drift, no old shared accounts, no former employees still listed in a system, no inherited role permissions nobody understands.

  Use that blank slate.

  Before staff handle PHI, the clinic should decide:

  Who needs EHR access?
  Who needs billing access?
  Who can see clinical notes?
  Who can export reports?
  Who can create or deactivate users?
  Who reviews audit logs?
  Who removes access after termination?

  Then give every workforce member a unique account and role-based access that matches the job. Shared logins may feel convenient during setup, but they damage the audit trail from the beginning. If five people use one login, the clinic cannot reliably show who viewed or changed what.

  Training should happen before access, not after the first busy week. The training record should show the person, role, date, content covered, and attestation. Verbal orientation without documentation is not a reliable compliance record.

  Operational takeaway: treat "training complete" and "unique account issued" as paired onboarding gates. No PHI access until both are documented.

post: |
  A new practice risk analysis should not be dramatic.

  It should be specific.

  The first version does not need to read like a hospital enterprise security report. For a one-location clinic, the scope may be manageable: EHR, billing system, scheduling, patient portal, email, phone or fax, laptops, mobile devices, backup, remote access, physical office, and vendors handling ePHI.

  The weak version says, "We use secure systems."

  The useful version asks:

  Where does ePHI live?
  Who can access it?
  What could reasonably go wrong?
  Which controls already exist?
  Which gaps have the highest likelihood and impact?
  Who owns each remediation?
  By what date?
  Where is the evidence stored?

  The risk analysis is not just a compliance artifact. It decides the next 90 days of work. If it finds no backup testing, that becomes a task. If it finds shared logins, that becomes a task. If it finds vendor uncertainty, that becomes a task.

  Operational takeaway: do the first risk analysis early enough that it can shape the clinic's setup decisions, not merely describe problems after launch.

  New clinics create HIPAA tasks before they have a HIPAA task system.

  That is how PHI-adjacent work ends up scattered across inboxes, sticky notes, personal reminders, text threads, generic task boards, and "ask me after lunch" memory.

  The work looks small at first:

  Confirm the EHR BAA.
  Post the NPP.
  Train the new receptionist.
  Review role permissions.
  Save the signed shredding agreement.
  Test backup restoration.
  Log the suspicious email report.
  Update the risk analysis with the new phone vendor.

  None of those tasks should require putting patient details into an unsafe place. But the task still needs ownership, due dates, evidence, and audit history.

  A PHI-safe task habit asks three questions before assigning the work:

  Can the task be written without patient identifiers?
  If evidence contains PHI, where should it be stored?
  Will we be able to prove completion later?

  PHIGuard is built for this kind of clinic compliance work: assigning, documenting, and retaining HIPAA operations without turning patient-adjacent tasks into generic project chatter.

  Operational takeaway: build the task hygiene before the clinic is busy. Once the schedule fills, scattered compliance work becomes normal unless the system is already in place.

post: |
  An incident log should exist before the first incident.

  That sounds obvious until a new clinic gets its first suspicious email, misdirected fax, lost device scare, portal access concern, or employee report and realizes the response process is still theoretical.

  The incident log does not mean something bad has happened. It means the clinic knows where to document the facts if something does.

  A starter incident log can be simple:

  Date discovered
  Reporter
  Incident type
  Systems or records potentially involved
  Immediate containment step
  Privacy/Security Officer notified
  Investigation owner
  Risk assessment status
  Outcome
  Evidence location
  Closure date

  The blank log is useful evidence too. It shows the process existed from the opening period, instead of being invented after the clinic needed it.

  Staff also need to know the reporting route. "Tell the office manager" is better than silence, but a documented process is stronger: what to report, how quickly, to whom, and what not to delete or alter while the incident is being reviewed.

  Operational takeaway: create the incident log in week one and train staff on how to use it. A response plan that nobody can find is not an operating procedure.

  A generic task board can make a new clinic feel organized while leaving the HIPAA program audit-fragile.

  The issue is not whether the board has columns.

  The issue is whether the clinic can safely manage PHI-adjacent work and later prove what happened.

  New practice setup creates a particular kind of task:

  "Confirm whether the billing service has a signed BAA."
  "Document that the new medical assistant completed HIPAA training."
  "Review access permissions before the EHR goes live."
  "Attach evidence from the backup test."
  "Track an incident investigation without exposing patient identifiers."

  Generic tools usually depend on local discipline. Someone has to remember not to paste PHI into comments. Someone has to decide where evidence belongs. Someone has to maintain the audit trail. Someone has to make recurring compliance reviews happen after opening.

  That may work for a tiny team for a few weeks. It gets brittle when the clinic starts hiring, seeing patients, changing vendors, and managing incidents.

  Operational takeaway: when evaluating a task system for a new clinic, do not ask only "Can it assign tasks?" Ask "Can it help us keep PHI out of the wrong place and retain evidence for compliance work?"

  Where do new clinics most often lose evidence: inboxes, shared drives, task comments, or someone's memory?

post: |
  The covered entity question should be answered before the startup checklist begins.

  For most new medical practices, the answer is straightforward: if the provider transmits health information electronically in connection with standard HIPAA transactions, such as electronic claims or eligibility checks, HIPAA applies as a covered healthcare provider.

  But the act of writing the answer matters.

  A clinic that documents its determination can then connect the rest of the program to that decision:

  We are a covered entity.
  Therefore we designated privacy and security responsibility.
  Therefore we prepared the Notice of Privacy Practices.
  Therefore we executed BAAs before vendor access.
  Therefore we trained workforce members before PHI access.
  Therefore we scoped the risk analysis around ePHI systems.

  A clinic that never writes the determination often builds HIPAA in fragments. The EHR has one compliance document. The billing vendor has another. The office manager has a folder of templates. Nobody can explain the program as a chain of obligations.

  Operational takeaway: start with the determination, then build the first 90 days from it. It gives the compliance file a spine.

post: |
  Vendor onboarding is part of HIPAA onboarding.

  New clinics often separate the two. Operations picks the EHR. Billing picks the clearinghouse. The owner picks the phone system. IT picks backup. Someone later asks compliance to "make sure we are covered."

  That sequence creates avoidable gaps.

  Every vendor intake should ask:

  Will this vendor create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI?
  What type of PHI?
  Does the vendor use subcontractors?
  Is a BAA required?
  Has the BAA been executed?
  Who approved access?
  Where is the signed copy stored?
  What is the renewal or review date?

  The goal is not to slow the clinic down. The goal is to prevent the opening month from becoming a pile of undocumented vendor exceptions.

  A practical rule for new practices: no PHI access until the vendor record has an owner, a BAA status, and an evidence location. If the answer is "we think the contract includes it," that is not enough. Confirm the BAA.

  Operational takeaway: make vendor intake a standard pre-access workflow. The first 90 days are the easiest time to make this habit normal.

  Your first access review should happen before access drift exists.

  New practices usually think of access reviews as a mature-clinic problem. But the first review is easiest when the roster is small and the systems are new.

  Pull the user list from each PHI system:

  EHR
  Practice management
  Billing
  Scheduling
  Patient portal
  Email
  Shared drive
  Backup or IT admin console

  For each user, verify:

  Name
  Role
  Systems accessed
  Permission level
  Business need
  Training completed
  Approval owner
  Date granted
  Deactivation trigger

  This is where setup mistakes become visible. The founding clinician has admin rights everywhere. The biller can see more clinical detail than needed. The temporary consultant still has access. The front desk account is shared because "we were just getting started."

  Catch those patterns before they become standard procedure.

  Operational takeaway: schedule a 60-day access review during pre-opening planning. It is much easier to correct permissions while the clinic is still forming its habits.

post: |
  A 90-day HIPAA review is not a ceremony.

  It is a gap hunt.

  By day 90, the clinic knows things it could not know before opening. Which intake steps staff actually follow. Which vendors were added. Which system settings were changed. Which training records are missing. Which policies sounded good on paper but do not match daily operations.

  The review should ask:

  Did any new ePHI system appear after launch?
  Did any vendor touch PHI without a completed record?
  Did every workforce member finish training before access?
  Are NPP acknowledgments tracked?
  Are incidents logged, including "no reportable breach" outcomes?
  Are access permissions still role-based?
  Does the risk analysis need updating?
  Can evidence be produced quickly?

  The output should not be shame. It should be assignments.

  Gap found.
  Owner named.
  Due date set.
  Evidence required.
  Completion retained.

  New clinics that do this at day 90 avoid a common first-year pattern: opening with decent intentions, then letting documentation decay as volume increases.

  Operational takeaway: put the 90-day review on the calendar before opening day. If it is not scheduled early, it will lose to patient volume later.

post: |
  The first incident response exercise should happen before the first real incident.

  New clinics can run a useful tabletop in 30 minutes.

  Pick a simple scenario: a staff member reports that an email containing patient information may have gone to the wrong recipient, or that a laptop used for clinic work is missing.

  Then walk the team through the operating questions:

  Who receives the report?
  What does the reporter do immediately?
  Who preserves evidence?
  Who determines what PHI may be involved?
  Who contacts IT or the vendor?
  Where is the incident logged?
  Who performs the risk assessment?
  Who decides whether notification obligations are triggered?
  What gets documented if the event is closed as not reportable?

  The point is not to make every employee a breach expert. The point is to make the first 15 minutes less chaotic.

  A tabletop also reveals whether policies are usable. If staff cannot find the incident form, do not know who the Security Officer is, or are unsure whether to delete a suspicious email, the procedure needs work.

  Operational takeaway: run one tabletop before day 90 and record what changed afterward. The improvement record is part of the evidence.

  What scenario would you use for a first tabletop: misdirected email, lost device, suspicious login, or patient complaint?

  A spreadsheet can start a HIPAA program.

  It rarely runs one well after the clinic gets busy.

  For a new practice, the first spreadsheet often looks reasonable: vendor list, BAA status, training dates, risk items, policy review dates, access review notes, incident log.

  The problem shows up when those rows become work.

  Who owns this remediation?
  Was the new hire trained before EHR access?
  Did the billing vendor BAA get signed?
  Where is the evidence?
  Who changed the status?
  What was overdue last month?
  Which incident items are still open?

  Spreadsheets are weak at accountability unless someone constantly maintains the accountability outside the spreadsheet. They also invite risky shortcuts when staff paste too much detail into cells because there is nowhere better to put context.

  PHIGuard is not generic project management with a healthcare label. It is built around small clinic compliance operations: BAA tracking, training records, risk work, incident tasks, policy review, audit history, and PHI-aware task handling.

  Operational takeaway: if your startup spreadsheet is becoming the system of record, decide early what belongs there, what needs a controlled evidence location, and what needs assigned compliance workflow.

post: |
  The Notice of Privacy Practices should not be a day-one scramble.

  For a new covered entity, the NPP is part of opening the clinic. Patients should receive it at the first service date, and the practice should make a good-faith effort to document acknowledgment.

  The operational failure is usually not that nobody has heard of the NPP. It is that nobody owns the intake evidence.

  The clinic prints the notice, but the website version is missing.
  Staff hand it out, but acknowledgments are not tracked.
  The EHR has a field, but nobody checks it.
  The policy says one thing, but front desk reality says another.

  A practical NPP setup record should include:

  Current NPP version
  Approval date
  Website posting location
  Waiting room posting location
  Intake script or workflow
  Acknowledgment capture method
  Exception process when a patient will not sign
  Owner for periodic review

  This is basic, but it matters. The first week of patient intake creates the habits the clinic may follow for years.

  Operational takeaway: test the NPP workflow before opening day by walking through a mock patient intake from arrival to acknowledgment record.

  The best compliance habit for a new clinic is boring: every gap becomes an owned task.

  Not a verbal reminder.
  Not a note in a meeting.
  Not "we should do that soon."

  An owned task.

  The first 90 days will surface gaps. A missing BAA. A training record without content. An access permission that is too broad. A backup test that has not been run. A policy that does not match how intake actually works. An incident form staff cannot find.

  The operating question is whether those gaps become traceable work.

  A good compliance task should include:

  Plain-language title without patient identifiers
  Requirement or source
  Owner
  Due date
  Priority
  Evidence needed
  PHI handling note
  Completion record
  Review or recurrence date

  This is where small clinics either build muscle or build debt. When the clinic is quiet, people can remember. When the phones start, remembering becomes a system failure.

  Operational takeaway: during the first 90 days, do not close a review meeting until every finding has an owner, due date, and evidence expectation. That habit is the compliance program becoming real.
