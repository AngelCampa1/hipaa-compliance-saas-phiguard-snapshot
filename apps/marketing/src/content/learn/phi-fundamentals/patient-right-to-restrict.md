The right to request restriction is the individual's right under 45 CFR § 164.522(a) to ask your clinic to restrict how it uses or discloses their PHI — specifically for treatment, payment, or healthcare operations purposes, or for disclosures to specific persons involved in their care. Most restriction requests are discretionary, meaning your clinic may decline them. But one restriction is mandatory, and small clinic staff frequently handle it incorrectly.

**Small-clinic example:** A patient at a 2-provider gynecology practice pays $180 in cash for an STI test and asks your front desk coordinator not to send any information to her insurance company. Your billing coordinator assumes this is a general preference and ignores it. But if the patient paid the full self-pay rate in cash, this is the mandatory out-of-pocket payment restriction under 45 CFR § 164.522(a)(1)(vi) — and you were required to honor it. Submitting a claim to her health plan after she requested the restriction is a Privacy Rule violation.

## The Regulatory Framework

Under 45 CFR § 164.522(a)(1), an individual has the right to request that your clinic restrict:

- Uses or disclosures of PHI for treatment, payment, or healthcare operations (TPO)
- Disclosures to persons involved in the individual's care under 45 CFR § 164.510(b) (family members or friends involved in care)

Your clinic is **not required** to agree to a restriction — with one critical exception described below.

If your clinic agrees to a restriction, the agreement is binding. You must honor the restriction in all subsequent uses and disclosures of the covered PHI, except where the restriction can be terminated under the specified circumstances.

## Voluntary Restriction Agreements

Most restriction requests are discretionary. A patient might request that their PHI not be shared with a particular family member on their emergency contact form, that a specific diagnosis not appear in coordination of benefits claims, or that their records not be provided to a former referring physician.

Your clinic may decline these requests. If you decline, you must inform the patient that you will not agree to the restriction.

If your clinic agrees, the restriction must be documented and honored. A violation of an agreed-upon restriction is a Privacy Rule violation — independent of whether the patient would have authorized the disclosure otherwise.

**What not to do:** Do not agree to restrictions your systems cannot enforce. A restriction your EHR cannot flag or your billing workflow cannot accommodate will be violated in the course of ordinary operations. Decline a restriction you cannot reliably honor rather than agree and unknowingly violate it.

## The Mandatory Restriction: Out-of-Pocket Payers

The operationally significant restriction is mandatory. Under 45 CFR § 164.522(a)(1)(vi), added by the HITECH Act and finalized in the 2013 Omnibus Rule, your clinic **must agree** to a restriction request when:

1. The disclosure would be to a health plan for purposes of payment or healthcare operations (not treatment), **and**
2. The PHI pertains solely to a healthcare item or service for which the individual — or someone other than the health plan — **paid your clinic out of pocket in full**

This provision protects patients who pay privately for sensitive services they do not want disclosed to their health plan. Common scenarios include reproductive health services, mental health counseling, substance use treatment, and STI testing.

**"Paid in full" means** the patient paid the complete charge — not a copay or coinsurance portion, but the full self-pay rate. A patient who pays a $150 copay while insurance covers $350 has not paid in full. A patient who pays your clinic's full self-pay rate of $150 for the visit has satisfied the mandatory restriction trigger.

**"Pertaining solely to" means** the restriction covers only the PHI that relates to the specifically paid-for service. If the patient paid out of pocket for a mental health consultation but has other services billed to insurance during the same encounter, the restriction applies only to the mental health consultation documentation — not the entire record.

**The restriction binds your business associates.** When you agree to the mandatory restriction, you must also instruct your billing company or clearinghouse not to submit a claim for the restricted service to the health plan. A restriction your billing company does not know about will be violated at claim submission.

## Documenting Restriction Agreements

When your clinic establishes a restriction agreement — voluntary or mandatory — document:

- Date of the restriction agreement
- Identity of the patient who requested the restriction
- The specific PHI or category of PHI covered by the restriction
- The specific uses and disclosures that are restricted
- Any exceptions to the restriction (such as emergency treatment)
- Date of any termination of the restriction

Documentation must be accessible to anyone who might use or disclose the covered PHI. A restriction documented in a compliance binder but not flagged in the EHR will be violated when billing staff process claims. The restriction is only as effective as the system that surfaces it at the point of use.

**Best practice for small clinics:**

1. Flag the restriction in the EHR patient record in a location visible to clinical and administrative staff
2. Note the restriction in the billing system so claims are suppressed accordingly
3. Include the restriction in the patient's designated record set so future access requests reflect it

## Terminating a Restriction Agreement

Under 45 CFR § 164.522(a)(2), restriction agreements terminate in these circumstances.

**By mutual agreement.** Your clinic may terminate a restriction if the patient agrees, either in writing or orally — with the oral agreement documented in writing.

**Prospectively by your clinic.** Your clinic may terminate a voluntary restriction it previously agreed to, but only going forward. The restriction continues to apply to PHI already used or disclosed under the agreement; only future uses and disclosures after the termination are freed.

**Emergency treatment.** An agreed-upon restriction may be overridden when emergency treatment requires disclosure of the restricted PHI. Your clinic must request that the receiving provider not further disclose the information.

**Key limitation:** Your clinic cannot retroactively disclaim a restriction it previously agreed to. The obligation persists for the PHI covered by the restriction even if honoring it has become operationally inconvenient.

## Operational Implications for Small Clinics

The mandatory out-of-pocket restriction most often affects small clinics in specialties where patients regularly pay privately for sensitive services — mental health, reproductive health, integrative medicine, and addiction treatment.

A practical workflow:

1. **At point of service:** When a patient pays the full self-pay rate, ask whether they want to invoke the mandatory restriction on health plan disclosure. Many patients who pay privately for sensitive services would invoke this right if they knew it existed.

2. **If the patient invokes the restriction:** Document it immediately in both the EHR and the billing system. Confirm with your billing staff or billing company that no claim will be submitted to the health plan for that service.

3. **Brief all billing and administrative staff.** Staff who process claims must know that claims for restricted services are not submitted to the health plan — regardless of what insurance information is on file.

4. **For multi-provider clinics and telehealth:** Ensure records flagged with a restriction are not shared with other clinicians through care coordination workflows unless sharing falls within the treatment exception or the restriction has been lifted.
