South Carolina medical clinics operating under HIPAA have a manageable state-law overlay compared to states with comprehensive health privacy statutes. The primary South Carolina-specific considerations are breach notification timing, state patient records access rights, and - for a narrow set of clinic structures - the South Carolina Insurance Data Security Act. This guide covers each area and translates them into action items for clinic administrators.

## HIPAA Baseline Requirements

Every South Carolina clinic that transmits health information electronically in connection with covered transactions is a HIPAA-covered entity subject to:

- A documented risk analysis and risk management program under 45 CFR Section  164.308(a)(1)
- Administrative, physical, and technical safeguards under 45 CFR Sections  164.308-164.316
- Business associate agreements with all vendors and contractors handling PHI, per 45 CFR Section  164.502(e)
- A Notice of Privacy Practices provided to patients at first service delivery
- Workforce training on privacy and security policies under 45 CFR Section  164.530(b)
- Breach notification procedures under the Breach Notification Rule at 45 CFR Part 164, Subpart D

South Carolina adds obligations in the specific areas described below.

## South Carolina Health Privacy Law Overview

South Carolina does not have a comprehensive state health privacy statute comparable to California's CMIA. The three statutes most relevant to clinic operations are:

**South Carolina Breach Notification Act (S.C. Code Section 39-1-90).** Any person who conducts business in South Carolina and owns, licenses, or maintains data containing personal information about South Carolina residents must notify affected residents of a security breach. The statute defines "personal information" as name combined with Social Security number, financial account information, or driver's license number. The notification must be made "in the most expedient time possible" and no later than 90 days after the breach is discovered, unless a law enforcement delay applies.

The 90-day state window is longer than HIPAA's 60-day ceiling. For HIPAA-covered entities, HIPAA's 60-day deadline controls. South Carolina's 90 days does not give you additional time beyond HIPAA's limit.

**S.C. Code Section 44-115-10 et seq. - Medical Records Access.** This South Carolina statute establishes patient rights to inspect and copy their medical records. Providers must respond to records access requests within defined timeframes and may charge fees subject to statutory limits. These rights run alongside HIPAA's access provisions at 45 CFR Section  164.524. When a South Carolina patient requests their records, your procedures must satisfy both the state statute and HIPAA.

**South Carolina Insurance Data Security Act (S.C. Code Section 38-99-10 et seq.).** This statute applies to entities regulated by the South Carolina Department of Insurance - primarily licensed insurers, not most medical clinics. Clinics structured as or affiliated with insurance licensees should assess applicability. The Act requires a written information security program, a risk assessment, and specific incident response procedures.

## Key Differences: South Carolina Law vs. HIPAA

| Topic | HIPAA | South Carolina Law |
|---|---|---|
| Breach notification deadline | 60 days from discovery (45 CFR Section  164.412) | 90 days from discovery (Section 39-1-90) - longer, HIPAA's 60 days controls |
| Patient records access | 30-day response deadline, with 30-day extension (45 CFR Section  164.524) | S.C. Code Section 44-115-10 - independent state-law access rights and response requirements |
| Health-specific state privacy law | HIPAA is the governing health privacy framework | No health-specific state law materially stricter than HIPAA for most clinics |
| Insurance data security | Not applicable | S.C. Code Section 38-99-10 - applies to insurance licensees only |
| Private right of action | Patients cannot sue directly under HIPAA | No explicit private right of action under Section 39-1-90; AG enforcement |
| Enforcement | OCR (federal) | South Carolina AG (state breach notification) |

The most operationally relevant South Carolina-specific requirement for most clinics is the state records access statute at Section 44-115-10. Your records request response procedures must account for both HIPAA's requirements and South Carolina's independent obligations.

## AG Enforcement in South Carolina

The South Carolina AG has authority to enforce the Breach Notification Act and pursue consumer protection violations. OCR retains independent authority to investigate and enforce HIPAA violations. A breach involving personal information defined under Section 39-1-90 can trigger parallel state and federal investigations.

South Carolina's enforcement environment does not provide a private right of action under the Breach Notification Act. Patient complaints feed into AG investigations rather than generating direct civil litigation under the breach statute. This does not eliminate common law tort exposure from a breach - negligence claims remain available - but the primary state enforcement mechanism runs through the AG.

## 5 Action Items for South Carolina Clinics

**1. Map your breach notification procedures to both HIPAA and Section 39-1-90.** For any breach involving PHI that also includes name plus Social Security number, financial account data, or driver's license number, both HIPAA and the South Carolina statute apply. Your incident response plan should identify this overlap, confirm HIPAA's 60-day deadline as the binding outer limit, and ensure notification letters satisfy both frameworks - typically HIPAA-compliant notification letters also satisfy Section 39-1-90 as long as they are sent within 60 days.

**2. Audit your patient records access procedures against S.C. Code Section 44-115-10.** Review your intake process for medical record access requests. Confirm that your procedures specify the timeframe for response, the permissible fees, and the format in which records may be provided. Reconcile these procedures with HIPAA's 30-day response standard and the South Carolina statute's requirements. Where the two frameworks differ on a specific point, apply the more protective standard.

**3. Assess whether the Insurance Data Security Act applies to your clinic structure.** If your clinic is a subsidiary of a health insurer, holds an insurance license in South Carolina, or operates a captive arrangement, the Insurance Data Security Act may apply. The Act requires a written information security program and an annual risk assessment. This overlaps substantially with HIPAA's Security Rule requirements, but the South Carolina Act has its own definitions and procedural requirements.

**4. Maintain current risk analysis documentation.** A documented, current risk analysis is the foundation of HIPAA Security Rule compliance and the starting point for any AG or OCR investigation. South Carolina has no health-specific privacy law beyond HIPAA for most clinics, so your HIPAA risk analysis is your primary compliance anchor. Complete the [HIPAA compliance self-assessment](/resources/hipaa-compliance-self-assessment) to identify gaps and maintain a current risk management plan.

**5. Confirm BAA terms address notification timing.** Your BAAs should require business associates to notify you of a breach promptly - a 10-day internal notification requirement is standard - so you can initiate your 60-day HIPAA notification clock without delay from slow-notifying vendors. Review existing BAAs to confirm this term is present and enforceable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**South Carolina's breach notification window is 90 days - does that mean I have more time than HIPAA allows**

Not for HIPAA-covered breaches. HIPAA's Breach Notification Rule at 45 CFR Section  164.412 requires covered entities to notify affected individuals within 60 days of discovering a breach. South Carolina's Breach Notification Act (Section 39-1-90) sets a 90-day window for state law purposes. For a breach that is covered by both HIPAA and South Carolina law, HIPAA's 60-day deadline is stricter and controls. The 90-day South Carolina window is longer, not shorter, so it does not tighten the deadline - but clinics should not rely on the 90-day state window and ignore HIPAA's 60-day ceiling.

**What are South Carolina patients' rights to their medical records under S.C. Code Section 44-115-10**

S.C. Code Section 44-115-10 et seq. gives patients the right to inspect and obtain copies of their medical records from South Carolina healthcare providers. The statute sets requirements for how providers must respond to record access requests, including time frames and permissible fees. These rights run alongside HIPAA's patient access requirements under 45 CFR Section  164.524, which generally give patients the right to access their records within 30 days. South Carolina clinics should ensure their records request procedures satisfy both the state statute and HIPAA's access requirements.

**Does the South Carolina Insurance Data Security Act apply to medical clinics**

The South Carolina Insurance Data Security Act (S.C. Code Section 38-99-10 et seq.) applies to entities licensed by the South Carolina Department of Insurance. Most medical clinics are not insurance licensees and are not subject to this statute. However, clinics that also hold an insurance license - such as clinics operating a captive insurance arrangement or that are subsidiaries of insurance holding companies - should assess whether the Insurance Data Security Act applies. The Act requires a written information security program and specific incident response procedures.
