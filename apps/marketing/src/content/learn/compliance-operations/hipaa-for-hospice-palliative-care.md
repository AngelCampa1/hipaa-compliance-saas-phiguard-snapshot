Hospice and palliative care exists at the edges of the healthcare system in ways that make HIPAA compliance harder, not easier. Care happens in living rooms and bedrooms. Family caregivers know more about the patient's daily condition than any clinician. Records continue to matter long after the patient has died. Each of these realities requires a deliberate compliance posture that office-based practices rarely need.

This guide is written for practice administrators at hospice agencies, home-based palliative care programs, and inpatient palliative care services.

## Why hospice and palliative care has unique HIPAA exposure

Hospice and palliative care practices encounter four HIPAA situations that primary care practices generally do not:

1. PHI is created and stored in private residences, where the practice cannot control physical access the way it controls a clinic.
2. Family members and friends are often the operational caregivers, which puts pressure on the personal-representative and family-involvement rules.
3. Patients frequently lack decision-making capacity, which shifts disclosure decisions to surrogates.
4. Most patients die during the relationship, and PHI of deceased individuals remains protected for 50 years.

Each situation has a defined HIPAA pathway. The goal is to walk that pathway intentionally rather than rely on improvisation by field staff.

## Top HIPAA risks for hospice and palliative care

The risks below repeatedly appear in compliance reviews of hospice agencies.

- Field-clinician laptops and tablets lost or stolen from vehicles without full-disk encryption.
- Paper visit notes left in patient homes or in clinician vehicles.
- Disclosures to family members who do not actually have personal-representative authority under state law.
- Bereavement mailings to family of deceased patients without an analysis of who may receive them.
- Photographs of patients used in marketing or memorial materials without authorization.
- Care-at-home platforms and remote monitoring devices deployed without a BAA.
- Volunteer programs given EHR access without workforce training and access controls.
- Spiritual care notes treated as outside the medical record when they are in fact PHI created on behalf of the practice.
- Texting between field staff and family on personal devices without an approved secure messaging tool.

## Vendor and BAA checklist for hospice and palliative care

Confirm a current BAA for each of the following where they apply to your operations:

- Hospice EHR and clinical documentation vendor.
- Remote patient monitoring devices and platforms.
- Pharmacy and infusion service partners.
- DME (durable medical equipment) supplier with EHR integration.
- Telehealth platform used for after-hours triage.
- Secure messaging platform for field staff.
- Bereavement program vendor or mailing house.
- Volunteer management software.
- Care-at-home coordination platform.
- IT managed service provider and mobile device management vendor.

## State law overlays affecting hospice and palliative care

State law layers heavily onto hospice operations. Personal representative status is determined by state law, including state-specific surrogate decision-making statutes that govern who may act when the patient lacks capacity and there is no advance directive. Several states have specific medical record retention rules for hospice programs that exceed HIPAA's six-year minimum and continue after the patient's death. State licensing rules for hospice agencies often include their own privacy and record-keeping requirements. Some states regulate disposal of medications left in the home after death, which can intersect with PHI on labels.

If your agency operates in multiple states, confirm the personal-representative rules in each state where you admit patients.

## HIPAA compliance checklist for hospice and palliative care

1. Document how your agency identifies personal representatives, including the state-law basis in each state of operation.
2. Encrypt every laptop, tablet, and phone used by field staff and require automatic lock after a short idle period.
3. Adopt a written policy for paper visit notes addressing transport, storage, and destruction.
4. Train field staff on the family-involvement rules in § 164.510(b) and on documenting patient agreement or disagreement.
5. Establish a procedure for bereavement contacts that respects the 50-year deceased-patient rule and any state law overlays.
6. Sign or refresh BAAs with every remote monitoring vendor, care-at-home platform, and DME supplier that touches PHI.
7. Treat volunteers who access PHI as workforce members: appoint them, train them, document their access, and offboard them.
8. Adopt an approved secure messaging platform and prohibit clinical communication via personal SMS or consumer messaging apps.
9. Audit photographs and memorial materials annually for authorization status and remove any that lack a current authorization.
10. Maintain an incident response runbook that addresses lost devices in the field, paper records left in homes, and disclosures to family members who turn out not to be personal representatives.
