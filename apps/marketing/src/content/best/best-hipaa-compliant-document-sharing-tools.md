## Decision summary

HIPAA-compliant document sharing is not just "send this file securely." For clinics, the real workflow is messier: intake packets, signed forms, referral documents, fax attachments, lab files, billing support, screenshots, vendor evidence, and exports from other systems.

The right tool should answer five questions:

- Who can access the file?
- How is the file shared outside the organization?
- Can sharing be limited, expired, revoked, or audited?
- Is the vendor relationship covered by the right agreement?
- What happens after the file is shared?

The last question is where many clinics lose control. File sharing gets the document from one place to another. It does not automatically assign the next task, verify the BAA, record the approval, or preserve why a decision was made.

## What to compare

| Criterion | Why it matters for PHI |
|---|---|
| BAA availability | A vendor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI for the clinic usually needs the right business associate agreement |
| Access controls | Staff should see only the PHI they need for their role |
| External sharing rules | Links, invitations, expiration, downloads, and forwarding need clear limits |
| Audit logs | The clinic should be able to review access and sharing activity |
| Retention and deletion | Files should not live forever just because nobody cleaned a folder |
| Workflow handoff | The system should support the task or record that comes after sharing |

Do not compare these products only by storage size. Storage is cheap. Mistakes around access, sharing, and follow-up are not.

| Product | Best fit | What stands out | Watch for |

| Dropbox Business | Teams already committed to Dropbox workflows | Familiar external sharing and file collaboration | Verify exact plan, agreement, and HIPAA-specific controls before PHI use |

## When a file-sharing tool is enough

A document-sharing tool can be enough when the workflow is simple: receive a file, store it in the right place, limit access, retain it correctly, and review the activity when needed.

That can fit a clinic with mature Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace governance. It can also fit teams using Box or ShareFile mainly for controlled exchange with patients, partners, or vendors.

But the tool has to be configured. A signed agreement and a secure-link feature do not fix messy permissions, unmanaged exports, or staff using personal accounts.

## When the clinic needs a workflow layer too

The clinic needs a workflow layer when files are tied to work that must be assigned, reviewed, or proved later. Examples:

- a vendor uploads evidence that requires BAA review
- a referral packet needs follow-up from a specific team member
- a fax attachment triggers an incident review
- a spreadsheet export needs disposal or retention tracking
- an intake form requires a documented response

That is where PHIGuard fits around the document layer. Use the file-sharing tool to store and move files. Use PHIGuard to keep ownership, due dates, evidence, vendor records, incidents, and review history connected.

## The practical recommendation

Choose the product that matches the file movement you actually have. If most sharing is external, Box or ShareFile may fit better. If the organization already lives in Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, tightening that environment may be more realistic than adding another file tool.

Just do not stop at storage. The clinic still needs to know who owns the next step after the file arrives.
