## Short Answer

Microsoft Planner can be part of a HIPAA-regulated Microsoft 365 environment when the clinic's license, tenant, Product Terms, DPA, and configuration are handled correctly. That does not make Planner a HIPAA compliance operations system.

Planner is for tasks, plans, boards, schedules, assignments, and project views inside Microsoft 365. A clinic still has to decide where it will keep the actual compliance record: signed BAAs, vendor review dates, incident notes, risk remediation, policy acknowledgements, training evidence, and audit history.

## Microsoft Planner vs PHIGuard

| Criterion | Microsoft Planner | PHIGuard |
|---|---|---|
| Primary use case | Microsoft 365 task and project planning | HIPAA compliance operations for clinics |
| BAA path | Microsoft BAA framework for in-scope cloud services and qualifying customer agreements | PHIGuard BAA and HIPAA-focused product workflow |
| Compliance templates | Generic plan templates and project views | Vendor BAAs, incidents, training, policies, risk, and evidence workflows |
| Audit evidence | Planner activity can appear in Microsoft 365 audit logs, but retention and review depend on licensing and admin process | Record-level history tied to compliance work |
| PHI workflow design | Clinic must decide what can go in task titles, comments, attachments, Teams, and email | Designed around patient-adjacent compliance tasks |
| Buying model | Included in some Microsoft 365 plans; premium Planner tiers are per-user add-ons | Clinic-oriented pricing |

## The BAA Is Real, But It Is Not the Whole Decision

Microsoft has a serious healthcare compliance posture. Its HIPAA materials explain that the BAA is available through Microsoft Product Terms and the Data Protection Addendum for customers covered by HIPAA using in-scope services. Microsoft also lists Planner in HIPAA/HITECH compliance materials for commercial cloud services.

For a clinic, that answers only the platform-contract question. It does not answer the workflow question.

Microsoft also says a BAA does not by itself make the customer's organization HIPAA compliant. If staff put patient names in task titles, attach screenshots from the EHR, discuss an incident in Teams comments, route notifications through email, or copy details into a plan description, the clinic still owns the end-to-end compliance design. The Microsoft BAA does not automatically organize the clinic's risk register, BAA tracker, incident log, training evidence, policy review calendar, and corrective actions.

## When Planner Is Fine

Planner can stay useful for:

- facilities tasks
- marketing and community outreach
- non-PHI administrative projects
- IT work that does not identify patients
- generic team follow-up inside Microsoft 365
- projects where Microsoft 365 governance is already mature

Planner may also be workable for larger organizations that already have Microsoft Purview, retention policies, DLP, audit log review, access governance, admin ownership, and written rules for what staff may put into tasks.

## Where Planner Becomes Weak for Small Clinics

Small clinics rarely have a dedicated Microsoft 365 compliance administrator. The practice manager, privacy officer, and operations lead may be the same person. That creates practical gaps:

- no obvious place for signed BAA evidence
- no vendor renewal workflow
- no incident classification flow
- no built-in training evidence loop
- no clinic-specific risk remediation register
- no simple way to tie policies, tasks, and evidence together
- too much room for PHI in task titles, comments, attachments, or Teams messages
- audit retention that may require E5, Microsoft Purview Suite, or add-on licensing for longer retention periods

Those gaps are not Microsoft failures. They are category-fit problems. Planner is a work planner; PHIGuard is a compliance operations workspace.

## What Changes With PHIGuard

PHIGuard gives clinics a narrower, more defensible system for the work that has to be proved later:

- vendor BAA tracker
- risk remediation tasks
- incident response records
- staff training follow-up
- policy review and acknowledgement
- access review evidence
- audit-ready task history

Use Planner for normal Microsoft 365 teamwork. Use PHIGuard when the task is part of the clinic's HIPAA evidence story.

## Buyer Checklist

Before using Microsoft Planner for any patient-adjacent workflow, confirm:

- the clinic's Microsoft agreement includes the applicable BAA terms
- Planner and the connected Microsoft 365 services are in scope for the tenant
- staff know what cannot go in task titles, descriptions, comments, attachments, and notifications
- Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive, Outlook, Loop, Power Automate, and Copilot features are separately reviewed where they touch the workflow
- audit logs and retention are configured for the evidence the clinic expects to produce
- guests, external sharing, and file permissions are locked down
- there is a separate system or documented process for BAAs, incidents, risk, training, and policy review

The right comparison is not "Planner or PHIGuard for every task." It is Planner for general work, PHIGuard for HIPAA work that needs a clean record.
