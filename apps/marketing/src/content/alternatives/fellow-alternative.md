## Short answer

Fellow is no longer a simple "no HIPAA path" comparison. Fellow's current help center says the product supports HIPAA compliance and that Fellow provides BAAs for organizations that require HIPAA compliance. It also says its AI features are covered by SOC 2 Type II, HIPAA, and GDPR compliance, and that Fellow does not use meeting data to train AI models.

That changes the clinic decision. The risk is not whether Fellow has any HIPAA language. The risk is whether the clinic has actually executed the BAA, configured AI and recording controls correctly, limited who can access recordings and transcripts, and decided which meeting content belongs in a meeting assistant at all.

PHIGuard is not a meeting recorder. It is the better home for the structured compliance work that meetings create: tasks, owners, evidence, incident follow-up, policy acknowledgements, vendor reviews, and risk remediation.

## Is Fellow HIPAA compliant?

Fellow can support HIPAA-governed use if the clinic handles the contractual and configuration details correctly.

Before any meeting may include PHI, verify:

| Requirement | What to confirm |
|---|---|
| BAA | Has the clinic signed Fellow's Business Associate Agreement? |
| Plan and workspace | Does the BAA cover the exact workspace, AI features, integrations, support workflow, and users involved? |
| AI settings | Which AI features are enabled, and can admins disable or restrict them? |
| Recording controls | Can staff pause, resume, redact, or prevent recording for sensitive meetings? |
| Access controls | Who can see recordings, transcripts, recaps, notes, and shared meeting libraries? |
| Subprocessors | Which AI, transcription, calendar, video, Slack, Teams, or other processors may receive meeting data? |
| Retention and deletion | How long are recordings, transcripts, notes, and biometric voice samples retained? |
| Staff rules | Do staff know which patient-specific details should not be spoken, recorded, summarized, or exported? |

If the clinic cannot answer these questions, it should not use Fellow for patient-adjacent meetings yet.

## Fellow vs PHIGuard

| Criterion | Fellow | PHIGuard |
|---|---|---|
| Primary design center | AI meeting notes, recordings, transcripts, agendas, recaps, and meeting action items | HIPAA compliance operations for clinics |
| BAA path | Fellow says BAAs are available for organizations that require HIPAA compliance | Built around BAA-covered clinic compliance workflows |
| Best use | Capturing and summarizing meetings when governance is approved | Running the follow-up work after compliance, incident, training, policy, or vendor meetings |
| AI surface | Meeting recording, transcription, summaries, Ask Fellow, uploads, memories, and AI settings | Compliance records, task ownership, evidence, and audit history |
| Clinic risk | Patient information can appear in audio, transcript, summaries, titles, action items, recordings, and integrations | Purpose-built for compliance tasks and evidence rather than broad meeting capture |
| Audit evidence | Meeting records may help context, but action tracking still needs governance | Tasks, assignments, incidents, policies, vendors, and evidence stay connected |

Fellow can be evaluated as a meeting assistant. PHIGuard should own the compliance record when the meeting creates obligations the clinic must prove later.

## Where Fellow can stay useful

Fellow may fit non-PHI meetings: leadership check-ins, operations planning, hiring meetings, marketing discussions, office projects, or general team management. It may also fit HIPAA-governed meetings after the clinic signs the BAA and configures access, AI, retention, recording, and integration controls.

The clinic should still decide which meetings are allowed to be recorded or transcribed. A care coordination huddle, breach review, or patient complaint meeting may create a sensitive record that needs tighter handling than a normal team update.

## Where Fellow becomes risky for clinics

Meeting tools create records quickly. A conversation that used to disappear after the meeting may become:

- a recording
- a transcript
- an AI summary
- a searchable recap
- an action item
- a calendar-linked note
- a recording-library entry
- a pushed task in another system

That is useful for productivity, but it increases the number of places PHI can appear. A patient name spoken out loud can enter the transcript. A billing dispute can become an action item. An incident review can become searchable by staff who do not need the context.

The clinic needs written rules before rollout: which meetings Fellow may join, which meetings must exclude the note taker, how to redact sensitive details, when recordings must be deleted, and where follow-up work belongs.

## What PHIGuard does instead

PHIGuard handles the action layer after a meeting:

- assign corrective actions from risk analysis reviews
- track incident response tasks and closeout evidence
- route policy acknowledgements after a policy change
- preserve training completion records
- track vendor BAA follow-up
- keep audit history around task ownership and completion

Those are the outputs clinic leaders need to prove. PHIGuard does not need to record the whole meeting to preserve the compliance work.

## Recommendation

Do not use the old claim that Fellow has no HIPAA path. Fellow now publishes HIPAA and BAA support.

Instead, treat Fellow as a meeting-capture tool that requires careful approval before PHI use. Treat PHIGuard as the system for compliance tasks and evidence after the meeting. Use the [incidental disclosure guide](/learn/phi-fundamentals/incidental-disclosure), [HIPAA task management guide](/learn/hipaa-basics/hipaa-task-management), [policy acknowledgement workflow](/learn/compliance-operations/hipaa-policy-acknowledgement-workflows), and [vendor BAA tracker](/resources/vendor-baa-tracker) to decide what belongs where.
