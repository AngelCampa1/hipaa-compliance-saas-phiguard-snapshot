Basecamp's flat-fee, opinionated design is genuinely appealing to small teams, and the per-company pricing model looks a lot like what PHIGuard does. The difference is the compliance layer. Basecamp does not sign Business Associate Agreements, has no HIPAA audit trail, and has no PHI-aware fields. For a clinic, that is a dealbreaker no matter how much you like the interface.

## The BAA Problem

37signals (Basecamp's maker) has stated publicly that Basecamp is not positioned for HIPAA use and will not sign a BAA. There is no premium tier that changes this. That means a clinic should not plan to run PHI-related workflows in Basecamp.

That distinction matters because Basecamp's simplicity can make it feel safer than a complicated enterprise system. A simple interface does not change the HIPAA analysis. If a project contains patient names, callback details, referral notes, incident facts, or screenshots from another clinical system, the clinic still needs a business associate contract, access controls, and audit evidence. Without those pieces, the workflow should be treated as out of scope for Basecamp.

The same issue applies to message boards, to-dos, comments, file uploads, and automatic email notifications. A clinic may start with a harmless office project and later add patient-specific details because the tool is convenient. The compliance failure is often not a dramatic breach; it is an ordinary work habit that gradually turns a general collaboration space into a PHI repository.

## What Changes With PHIGuard

PHIGuard keeps the clinic-oriented packaging you would want from a simpler operational tool, and adds the compliance layer Basecamp does not offer. PHIGuard includes:

- **Signed BAA** available from day one and completed during onboarding
- **Immutable audit trail** designed to support audit-control documentation
- **PHI-aware fields** that keep patient data out of notifications and logs
- **Compliance templates** for annual training, risk analysis, incident response, and policy review

## Evaluation Criteria for Clinics

Before keeping a workflow in Basecamp, ask whether the work can be completed without patient identifiers, treatment facts, billing details, appointment details, or compliance evidence tied to a specific person or incident. If the answer is no, the workflow belongs somewhere else. A clinic should also review whether file uploads, forwarded emails, project messages, and third-party integrations could expose PHI even when the project title looks administrative.

PHIGuard is the better fit when the clinic needs to prove who owned a compliance task, when it was completed, what evidence was attached, and what changed after review. Those records become important during an OCR inquiry, payer audit, malpractice review, or internal incident investigation. Basecamp can coordinate general teamwork; PHIGuard is meant to preserve the compliance trail.

## Who Should Use PHIGuard Instead of Basecamp

Clinics that like the Basecamp philosophy — one flat price, no per-seat games — but need an actual BAA and audit trail will find PHIGuard closer to the spirit of Basecamp than any enterprise plan from a competitor.

## Evaluation Notes for Clinic Buyers

Basecamp can be a reasonable operational tool when the workflow is clearly non-clinical, but patient-adjacent work changes the buying criteria. A clinic needs a signed BAA when PHI is involved, a documented scope for which features are covered, and a way to prove who accessed or changed compliance records. The source set for this page is HHS: Business Associates. Treat those pages as starting points, then confirm plan names, BAA eligibility, and support commitments in the current contract before relying on the tool for PHI.

When comparing Basecamp with PHIGuard, separate general productivity from HIPAA operations. Basecamp keeps teamwork simple, but it does not offer the HIPAA contractual and operational safeguards a clinic needs for regulated work. Basecamp is not positioned as a HIPAA tool and should not be treated as a place to run PHI-related workflows without a BAA. Clinics should verify current vendor terms directly, but the practical planning assumption is that Basecamp is for non-PHI work. PHIGuard is most relevant when the work involves incident logs, vendor BAAs, policy acknowledgements, training evidence, recurring risk-analysis tasks, and review history that needs to be defensible later.

Keep Basecamp for workflows where it is strongest: planning, collaboration, sales operations, internal tracking, or non-PHI documentation. Move the compliance record to a system designed for clinic accountability when the task names a patient, describes care, tracks an incident, stores a BAA, or requires a dated record of staff action. That split lets teams avoid overbuying enterprise software while keeping HIPAA evidence in one controlled place.
