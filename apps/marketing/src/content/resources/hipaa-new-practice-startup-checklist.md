Most new practices open with a clinical plan and a billing plan, then discover six months later that their HIPAA program is a folder of half-finished templates. This checklist is the version a practice administrator can hand to the founding clinician and actually finish.

The structure is phased so it matches how a clinic actually opens. Items are grouped into pre-opening, first 30 days, 60-day review, and 90-day review. Each item maps to a HIPAA citation so you know why it is on the list, not just that someone said so.

## Pre-opening

Before the first patient encounter, finish the items that the Privacy Rule and Security Rule require to be in place when PHI starts moving. Confirm covered entity status. Designate a Privacy Officer and a Security Officer per 45 CFR 164.530(a) and 45 CFR 164.308(a)(2) — one person can hold both roles in a small practice. Draft the Notice of Privacy Practices. Sign the BAA with your EHR vendor before the EHR holds any PHI. Conduct an initial risk analysis under 45 CFR 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A) — even a short, documented one is better than the absence of one.

## First 30 days

Document workforce training under 45 CFR 164.530(b). Training that is not documented does not exist as far as an auditor is concerned. Post the NPP in the waiting area and on the website. Distribute the NPP to patients at first encounter and document acknowledgement. Adopt a sanction policy for workforce members who violate the policies. Stand up a complaint procedure with a named contact. Inventory every vendor that will touch PHI and confirm a signed BAA is on file before access begins.

## 60-day review

Write an incident response and breach notification plan that maps to 45 CFR 164.400 through 164.414. Review who has access to which systems and confirm the access matches the minimum necessary standard. Audit the training records — who completed what, on what date. Draft a contingency plan including data backup, disaster recovery, and emergency mode operation per 45 CFR 164.308(a)(7).

## 90-day review

Finalize the full risk analysis documentation. Organize the evidence binder so any item on the checklist can be produced in under five minutes. Set the annual review calendar: NPP review, risk analysis refresh, training renewal, BAA renewal dates, contingency plan test. The 90-day mark is also the right time to run a tabletop exercise on the incident response plan with the whole team.

A clinic that finishes this checklist in 90 days has a defensible compliance program. Not a perfect one — those do not exist — but one that holds up to a complaint-driven OCR inquiry without scrambling.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the New Practice Startup checklist as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes Pre-opening tasks: covered entity determination, Privacy and Security Officers, NPP draft, EHR BAA, initial risk analysis; First 30 days: documented workforce training, NPP posted and distributed, sanction policy, complaint procedure; 60-day review: incident response plan, access controls reviewed, training records audited, contingency plan in place; 90-day review: full risk analysis documentation, evidence binder organized, annual review calendar set. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

PHIGuard positions this kind of artifact as part of day-to-day compliance operations: a task, an owner, evidence, and a repeatable review cadence. The goal is not to create more paperwork. The goal is to make the clinic able to show, with dated records, how it applied HIPAA requirements to the workflow in front of it.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
