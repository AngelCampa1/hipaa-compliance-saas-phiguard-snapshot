A PHI disposal log is one of the smallest documents in a compliance program and one of the first things a surveyor or OCR investigator asks for. Most small clinics destroy PHI every week — old intake forms, sign-in sheets, fax confirmations, dead hard drives, retired phones. Without a log, none of that destruction is provable.

HIPAA does not prescribe a specific form, but 45 CFR 164.310(d)(2)(i) requires policies and procedures for the final disposition of PHI and the media on which it is stored. 45 CFR 164.530(c) requires reasonable administrative, technical, and physical safeguards. A written log is how you prove both happened.

## What the log captures

Every row records a single destruction event. The fields are deliberately minimal so staff actually fill them in:

- Date of destruction
- Description of the item or media (do not include patient identifiers — write "intake forms 2024-Q1" not patient names)
- Volume or count
- Destruction method (cross-cut shred, certified vendor pickup, NIST SP 800-88 wipe, physical destruction of drive)
- Destroyer initials
- Witness initials
- Vendor name and BAA on file (if outsourced)

The witness column matters. Two-person verification is the standard practice for paper destruction in a clinic setting and the easiest control to demonstrate during an audit.

## Acceptable destruction methods

Paper PHI: cross-cut shredding to particles no larger than roughly 1 by 5 millimeters, or use of a certified destruction vendor under a signed BAA. Strip-cut shredders are not sufficient.

Electronic media: follow NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 1 guidance. For drives leaving the clinic, prefer physical destruction or certified Purge. For drives being reused inside the clinic, a verified Clear is acceptable. Mobile devices that touched PHI need a documented factory reset plus device wipe — and a log entry.

Vendor pickups: keep the certificate of destruction with the log entry. The certificate plus the BAA plus the log row is the full audit trail.

## Retaining the log itself

The disposal log is HIPAA documentation. Keep it for six years from creation or last effective date per 45 CFR 164.530(j)(2). Do not store the log in the same bin you destroy. Many clinics keep the current year on a clipboard at the shred station and archive completed years to the compliance binder or document store.

## How clinics use this template

Print the PDF and clip it to the shred bin or filing cabinet. When PHI gets destroyed, the staff member fills the row and a witness initials. At month end the practice administrator scans the page into the compliance evidence folder. That single habit closes one of the most commonly cited gaps in OCR resolution agreements: undocumented disposal.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the PHI Disposal & Destruction Tracking Log as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes Required log fields aligned to 45 CFR 164.310(d)(2)(i)-(ii) media disposal; Acceptable destruction methods for paper and electronic media (NIST SP 800-88 references); Witness and destroyer signature columns with date and item description; Vendor BAA tracking for outsourced shredding and certified destruction services. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

PHIGuard positions this kind of artifact as part of day-to-day compliance operations: a task, an owner, evidence, and a repeatable review cadence. The goal is not to create more paperwork. The goal is to make the clinic able to show, with dated records, how it applied HIPAA requirements to the workflow in front of it.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
