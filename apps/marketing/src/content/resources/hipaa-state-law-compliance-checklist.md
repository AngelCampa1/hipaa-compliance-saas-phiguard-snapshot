## Why State Law Adds a Layer Your HIPAA Program May Not Cover

HIPAA is a federal statute that sets minimum standards for protected health information. The preemption rule at 45 CFR § 160.203 is often misunderstood: HIPAA does not override state law — it coexists with it. When a state law is more protective of patient privacy, the state law applies. When the state law is less protective, HIPAA applies. For a small medical clinic, your compliance program has two floors, not one.

The practical consequences are real. California's breach notification law requires covered entities to notify affected individuals within 72 hours of discovering a breach, not 60 calendar days. Texas requires notification within 60 days but mandates notification to the Texas Attorney General for breaches affecting 250 or more Texas residents. New York's SHIELD Act and HITECH-aligned statute impose specific technical safeguard requirements beyond the HIPAA Security Rule baseline. A HIPAA-only compliance program misses all of this.

This checklist is a downloadable PDF organized by state. It covers ten states selected for a combination of their population size, the frequency with which their laws differ materially from HIPAA, and the enforcement activity of their state Attorneys General. If your clinic operates in any of these states — or if you have patients whose records you hold as a covered entity operating in multiple states — this resource gives you a side-by-side view of where your program needs state-specific additions.

## What's in the PDF

### Section 1 — Breach Notification Requirements by State

Each state entry includes:

- **Notification deadline to individuals.** How many days from discovery does the covered entity have to notify affected individuals Federal HIPAA gives 60 calendar days from discovery of the breach. Several states in this checklist require action in 30 to 45 days.
- **Notification threshold.** Some states require notification for any breach of individually identifiable health information, including paper records, not just ePHI covered by HIPAA's Breach Notification Rule.
- **Notification to state regulator.** Most states require notification to the AG, the Department of Health, or both when a breach exceeds a threshold number of affected state residents. This section lists the threshold and the regulator for each of the ten states.
- **Statutory citation.** The exact section of state code governing breach notification, so your compliance counsel can pull the primary source.

### Section 2 — Additional Consent Requirements

HIPAA permits many disclosures without patient authorization: treatment, payment, and health care operations; public health; required by law; and others. Several states add consent requirements on top of HIPAA's permission structure. Examples:

- Some states require patient authorization for disclosures to employers that HIPAA would permit under the "health care operations" exception.
- Some states require written authorization for disclosures of substance use disorder records to other treating providers, even when 42 CFR Part 2 (the federal substance use confidentiality rule) might not require it.
- Some states impose additional requirements on the content and format of authorizations beyond what HIPAA specifies.

This section identifies where each of the ten states imposes requirements beyond HIPAA's baseline and what those requirements are.

### Section 3 — Mental Health Records Protections

Mental health records occupy a complex intersection of federal and state law. HIPAA covers mental health records as PHI. Many states have enacted stricter confidentiality protections that apply additional restrictions on who can access mental health records, when those records can be disclosed without authorization, and how long records must be retained separately from other medical records.

For each state, this section covers: whether the state has a distinct mental health records confidentiality statute, the key restrictions that go beyond HIPAA, and any special rules for psychotherapy notes (which HIPAA already treats as a separate category with heightened protections under 45 CFR § 164.524(a)(1)(ii)).

### Section 4 — Genetic Information Restrictions

HIPAA and the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) both address genetic information, but state law in several states is more restrictive — particularly around insurer access to genetic test results and employer access to genetic information. This section identifies which of the ten states impose restrictions relevant to covered entities, including restrictions on disclosures to third parties that HIPAA's minimum necessary standard alone would not prevent.

### Section 5 — State AG Contact Information and Enforcement History

When a breach occurs, you may need to notify the state Attorney General simultaneously with your HHS notification. This section provides current AG contact information for each of the ten states, the AG office's designated health privacy enforcement unit where one exists, and a brief note on the AG's enforcement posture. Some state AGs pursue health privacy violations independently and aggressively; others defer to HHS. Knowing the enforcement climate helps you calibrate your response.

## Who Should Use This Resource

This checklist is designed for:

- **Privacy Officers** at clinics operating in one or more of the ten covered states, who are responsible for maintaining a current understanding of applicable state law.
- **Practice Administrators** who are reviewing existing compliance policies and want to verify that state-specific requirements are addressed.
- **Small clinic compliance programs** that do not have in-house legal counsel and need a structured starting point before engaging outside compliance counsel for a full state law review.

It is not a substitute for legal advice when your clinic faces an actual breach, complaint, or AG inquiry. It is a practical reference tool for the ongoing maintenance of your compliance program.

## How to Use This Checklist

**Step 1 — Identify the states that apply to you.** If your clinic is physically located in one of the ten states, that state's rules apply. If your clinic operates in multiple states (e.g., a telehealth practice licensed in several states), every state where you hold records of patients who received services in that state may apply.

**Step 2 — Compare your current breach response procedures to the state's notification deadline.** If your breach response plan defaults to the 60-day HIPAA window and your state requires 30 or 45 days, update your internal breach timeline.

**Step 3 — Review your Notice of Privacy Practices and authorization forms.** If your state requires additional consent for disclosures that HIPAA permits without authorization, your NPP and authorization forms need to reflect that. Your Privacy Officer should flag specific disclosure categories for legal review.

**Step 4 — Verify your mental health and substance use disorder records procedures.** If your clinic provides behavioral health services, confirm that your records access controls and disclosure procedures comply with both the federal baseline and the applicable state statute.

**Step 5 — Add your state AG's contact information to your breach response plan.** The breach response plan should include the AG notification threshold and the specific office contact so that when you are managing an active breach you are not searching for contact information at the same time.

**Step 6 — Document your review.** Date the review, record who completed it, and file it with your compliance documentation. A documented state law review is evidence of an active compliance program.

## Regulatory Basis

The HIPAA preemption framework is established at 45 CFR § 160.203. The general rule is that HIPAA preempts contrary state law. The exception: if the state law "relates to the privacy of individually identifiable health information and is more stringent" than HIPAA, the state law is not preempted (§ 160.203(b)). "More stringent" is defined at 45 CFR § 160.202 and includes requirements that provide greater privacy protections, grant patients greater rights of access or amendment, or require covered entities to retain more information.

HHS has published preemption guidance at hhs.gov. The guidance makes clear that covered entities must independently analyze state law — HHS does not publish a definitive preemption determination for every state statute.
