## Who Needs This Template

Any covered entity that receives requests to release patient records needs a compliant authorization form on file. That includes medical clinics, behavioral health practices, physical therapy offices, and any other healthcare provider that maintains a designated record set. If your staff are currently adapting a form they found online years ago, or using the EHR vendor's default form without verifying it meets federal requirements, this template is the starting point for a correction.

A missing or deficient authorization is one of the most common findings in OCR investigations of small practices. Under 45 CFR §164.508, the form must contain eight specific elements. An authorization missing even one of them is legally invalid, and proceeding on an invalid authorization is an impermissible disclosure.

## What HIPAA Rule It Satisfies

The HIPAA Privacy Rule, codified at 45 CFR §164.508, governs when a covered entity may use or disclose PHI based on a patient's written authorization. The rule specifies exactly what an authorization must contain, when one is required versus optional, and what rights patients retain after signing.

Authorizations are required for any disclosure not covered by the treatment/payment/operations exception, and are always required for disclosures of psychotherapy notes, marketing, and sale of PHI. For standard records releases requested by the patient or a patient-designated recipient, a properly completed §164.508 authorization is your legal basis for proceeding.

## What Is Inside This Template

The form is organized to capture all eight required elements in a patient-readable format:

**Section 1 — Patient identification.** Full name, date of birth, and any internal identifier your clinic uses.

**Section 2 — Description of the information to be used or disclosed.** The form prompts for specific record types (visit notes, lab results, imaging, billing records, complete chart) and a date range. Vague descriptions like "all records" without a time frame are a common deficiency.

**Section 3 — Person or entity authorized to make the disclosure.** Your clinic name and contact information.

**Section 4 — Person or entity to whom the information will be disclosed.** Name and address of the receiving party. Required. A release addressed to "whomever it may concern" does not satisfy this element.

**Section 5 — Purpose of the disclosure.** Either "at the request of the individual" or a specific stated purpose. The patient may decline to state a purpose, but the form must offer the option.

**Section 6 — Expiration date or event.** Either a calendar date or a natural endpoint ("upon conclusion of treatment," "one year from signature"). An authorization with no expiration is invalid.

**Section 7 — Signature and date.** The patient's signature and date. If a personal representative signs, the representative's authority must be documented.

**Section 8 — Required statements.** The right to revoke, any restrictions on revocation, the possibility of re-disclosure by the recipient, and a statement that treatment is not conditioned on signing (unless it is for research-related treatment or certain insurance enrollment situations).

## How to Fill It Out

Work through each section with the patient or their representative before accepting the form. Do not accept an incomplete form — stamp it "deficient" and return it with a written note identifying what is missing.

**Step 1.** Confirm the patient's identity using two identifiers before completing Section 1.

**Step 2.** Help the patient describe the specific records and date range in Section 2. If the request is too broad, narrow it to the minimum necessary for the stated purpose.

**Step 3.** Confirm the receiving party's name and current mailing or secure fax number in Section 4. Do not release records until you have a verified destination.

**Step 4.** Review the expiration field. If the patient leaves it blank, prompt them to either enter a date or circle an event description.

**Step 5.** Have the patient sign and date in your presence if possible. If the form was mailed in, compare the signature to your file.

**Step 6.** Keep the original in the patient record. Provide the patient with a copy on request. Log the release in your disclosure accounting if required.

## Who Fills It Out and How to Store It

**Who fills it out:** Front-desk or medical records staff complete Sections 1 through 4 with the patient or their authorized representative. The patient completes Section 5 (purpose) and Section 7 (signature). All eight elements must be present before the form is accepted.

**Where to store it:** Keep the original signed authorization in the patient's designated record set for the duration required by your records retention schedule, but no less than six years from the date of creation or the date it was last in effect, per 45 CFR §164.530(j). A copy of the authorization must also be filed in the disclosure accounting log if the disclosure is one the patient could request an accounting of under §164.528.

**How often the form is updated:** Review the form at least annually and update it any time the following occur: a change to your state's ROI laws; a change to federal regulations affecting the required elements of §164.508(c); a change to your clinic's internal process for handling records requests; or any OCR guidance that identifies a common deficiency in authorization form design. The effective date on the form must match your current version.

**What triggers an immediate update:** OCR enforcement actions against a form element, a court finding that a form was deficient, a state law amendment that adds a required disclosure, or any regulatory update to 45 CFR §164.508.
