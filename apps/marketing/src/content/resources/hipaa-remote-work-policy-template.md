## Why You Need a Written Policy

A clinic that allows any work outside the building — a biller working claims from home, a provider charting after hours on a laptop, an administrator answering a portal message from their phone — has extended ePHI access beyond the facility. Under 45 CFR § 164.310(c), workstation security applies to every workstation that accesses ePHI, regardless of where it sits. Under § 164.312, technical safeguards including access control, audit controls, integrity, person-or-entity authentication, and transmission security apply to every device that touches ePHI.

A written remote work and BYOD policy turns those abstract requirements into specific rules that a workforce member can read, sign, and follow. Without a policy, every remote work decision is an ad-hoc judgment call by an individual employee. With a policy, the clinic has documented its expectations, the workforce member has acknowledged them, and an OCR investigator has a record to review.

## Who Should Use This Template

Practice administrators, HIPAA security officers, and small-clinic owners who allow any form of remote work — full-time remote, hybrid, occasional after-hours work, or BYOD — and who do not yet have a written policy. The template is designed to be adopted with light edits: replace the bracketed clinic name, confirm your specific tools and vendors, and have the policy reviewed by counsel before it becomes part of your employee handbook.

## What the Policy Covers

The template includes a purpose statement tied to the Security Rule, a scope section that names which workforce members and which devices are covered, an eligibility section that ties remote work approval to specific roles and supervisor sign-off, an approved devices section that distinguishes clinic-issued from BYOD with separate requirements for each, a required configuration section that lists encryption, MFA, auto-lock, supported operating systems, and mobile device management, an acceptable use section that names what staff may and may not do in the remote environment, a lost or stolen device section with same-day reporting requirements, and a termination of remote access section that ties to your offboarding checklist. It closes with an acknowledgment signature page.

## Adoption Steps

Read the full PDF, edit the bracketed fields to match your clinic, route it through counsel, present it at a staff meeting alongside a brief training session, collect signed acknowledgments, and file the signed copies in each workforce member's personnel record. Re-issue the acknowledgment annually and any time the policy materially changes. Store the policy in the same compliance repository as your other Security Rule policies so an auditor can find it without asking.

## What This Template Does Not Replace

A policy is not a substitute for technical controls. The clinic still has to actually deploy mobile device management, actually require MFA on the EHR, and actually enforce VPN-only access to PHI from outside the office. The policy is the governance layer; the technical safeguards are the implementation. Both are required under the Security Rule. Adopting this template gives you the governance artifact and the workforce acknowledgment; the implementation work happens in your IT vendor relationships and your EHR configuration.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the Remote Work & BYOD Policy template as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes Eligible roles and supervisor approval — who may work remotely and under what conditions; Approved devices — clinic-issued versus BYOD with specific configuration requirements; Required controls — full-disk encryption, MFA, auto-lock, and supported operating systems; Acceptable use — no public Wi-Fi without VPN, no PHI in personal cloud accounts or email. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

PHIGuard positions this kind of artifact as part of day-to-day compliance operations: a task, an owner, evidence, and a repeatable review cadence. The goal is not to create more paperwork. The goal is to make the clinic able to show, with dated records, how it applied HIPAA requirements to the workflow in front of it.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
