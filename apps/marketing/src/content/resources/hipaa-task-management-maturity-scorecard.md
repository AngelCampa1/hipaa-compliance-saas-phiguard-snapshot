## Why Maturity Scoring Matters for Compliance Programs

Most small clinic compliance programs are not absent. They're inconsistent. Training happens, but only when someone remembers. BAAs get signed, but no one tracks expiration dates. Incidents get reported, but some near-misses don't make it to the log. Access gets provisioned, but terminations sometimes take two weeks to revoke.

A maturity scoring approach accepts this reality and gives administrators a structured way to assess where their program actually stands, not where they believe it stands. The gap between "we do this" and "we do this consistently, with documented evidence" is the compliance gap most OCR investigations reveal.

This scorecard measures your program across six dimensions on a 1-to-5 scale. It takes approximately 30 minutes to complete honestly. The result is a profile of your program's strengths and the dimensions where investment will reduce the most risk.

## How to Use This Scorecard

Read each dimension's level descriptions carefully. Select the level that most accurately describes your clinic's current state: not your aspirational state, not your policy state, but your operational reality.

A Level 3 ("Defined") program has documented processes, follows them consistently, and produces evidence artifacts. A Level 2 ("Repeatable") program gets the right things done, but inconsistently, informally, or only because one person carries the program in their head.

Score honestly. A program scored at Level 2 that correctly identifies gaps and closes them is more defensible than a program scored at Level 4 that can't produce the evidence.

## The Six Dimensions

### Dimension 1: Task Ownership and Accountability

This dimension measures whether compliance tasks have named owners, deadlines, and completion tracking - or whether compliance is a background expectation with no assigned responsibility.

**Level 1 - Ad Hoc:** No formal compliance calendar or task list. Compliance tasks happen reactively: when a vendor asks for a BAA, when a staff member raises a question, or when an incident occurs. No one person owns the compliance program.

**Level 2 - Repeatable:** One person (usually the practice administrator or the Privacy Officer, if they're the same person) tracks compliance tasks mentally or in a personal spreadsheet. Tasks mostly get done, but there's no visibility into what's overdue or what's been completed.

**Level 3 - Defined:** A documented compliance calendar exists with named tasks, deadlines, and owners. The calendar is reviewed at least quarterly. Task completion is logged.

**Level 4 - Managed:** Tasks are assigned in a tracking system with due dates, reminders, and completion timestamps. The practice administrator can generate a report showing which tasks are complete, overdue, or upcoming. Backup owners are designated for tasks with hard deadlines.

**Level 5 - Optimized:** Compliance tasks cascade automatically from regulatory triggers (e.g., a new vendor added → BAA task triggered; a staff member terminated → access revocation task triggered). Task completion rates and cycle times are measured. Gaps surface automatically rather than on discovery.

### Dimension 2: Evidence Documentation

This dimension measures whether your clinic can produce evidence of compliance activities - not just assert that they happened.

**Level 1:** No systematic evidence retention. If asked to show when training last occurred, the administrator would have to check email threads and their own recollection.

**Level 2:** Some evidence exists - perhaps a sign-in sheet from last year's training session, a folder with some BAA PDFs - but it's disorganized and incomplete. Producing evidence takes significant effort and may reveal gaps.

**Level 3:** Evidence is produced for each required activity and stored in a defined location. Training logs, signed acknowledgments, access review records, and incident reports are filed in a structure that allows retrieval within a reasonable time.

**Level 4:** Evidence is linked to the task that produced it. When a quarterly access review is completed, the signed review log is attached to the task record. Evidence can be filtered by type, date range, and activity. The clinic can produce a complete compliance record for any 12-month period in under an hour.

**Level 5:** Evidence is automatically captured as a byproduct of compliance operations. When a user's access is revoked in a system, the revocation event is logged with a timestamp and linked to the offboarding task. Human intervention to create evidence artifacts is minimized.

### Dimension 3: Vendor and BAA Oversight

This dimension measures whether the clinic tracks its business associate relationships systematically and keeps BAAs current.

**Level 1:** BAA inventory consists of individual BAA documents in a folder, possibly incomplete. The clinic does not know which vendors have signed BAAs and which have not. BAA expiration dates are not tracked.

**Level 2:** An informal list of vendors with BAAs exists. Most BAAs were signed, but tracking is inconsistent. Some vendors were added recently without a completed BAA review. Expiration tracking is ad hoc.

**Level 3:** A documented vendor inventory lists all vendors, their BAA status (signed/pending/not required), the BAA execution date, and the renewal date. The inventory is reviewed at least annually. New vendors are added to the inventory before system access is granted.

**Level 4:** BAA renewals are tracked with 90-day advance reminders. The clinic has documented vendor risk assessments for each vendor's security posture. New vendor additions trigger a defined BAA review process with a checklist.

**Level 5:** Vendor reviews include assessment of subprocessors, AI feature coverage, and data residency. BAA renewal triggers a formal re-assessment of the vendor's security practices, not just a signature. The clinic maintains a complete chain of subprocessor documentation from all primary vendors.

### Dimension 4: Workforce Training

This dimension measures whether all workforce members receive documented HIPAA training on a defined schedule.

**Level 1:** Training happens informally during onboarding but is not documented. No annual refresher training occurs. Contractors and locums receive no training.

**Level 2:** Annual training is conducted but incompletely documented. Attendance is tracked by memory or an informal sign-in sheet. Contractors sometimes receive training; sometimes they don't. Training content is not standardized.

**Level 3:** Annual training is conducted, completed by all workforce members including contractors and locums, and documented with dates, method, and verifier. New hires receive training before PHI access is granted. Documentation is filed in a central location.

**Level 4:** Training completion is tracked with automated reminders for staff who haven't completed it. Training content is role-specific - clinical staff receive different modules than billing staff. Assessment scores are recorded. Remedial training is triggered for failed assessments.

**Level 5:** Training completion and assessment data are analyzed to identify gaps. Training content is updated annually based on actual incident patterns, regulatory guidance updates, and staff assessment results. Training records are integrated with workforce access records.

### Dimension 5: Incident Handling

This dimension measures whether the clinic identifies, logs, triages, and resolves HIPAA incidents and security events consistently.

**Level 1:** Incidents are not formally tracked. Staff are not sure who to notify if they suspect an incident. Some incidents may not have been reported to the Privacy Officer at all.

**Level 2:** Staff know the Privacy Officer is the contact for incident reporting. Some incidents are logged, but the log is incomplete. The 4-factor breach risk assessment is not consistently applied. Near-misses are rarely reported.

**Level 3:** A defined incident reporting process exists and has been communicated to all workforce members. Incidents and near-misses are logged in a consistent format. The 4-factor risk assessment is applied to each incident. Breach notification decisions are documented.

**Level 4:** Incident intake uses a structured form (who reported it, when, what happened, what PHI was involved, immediate containment steps taken). The triage process is documented with escalation criteria. Incident response times are tracked.

**Level 5:** Incident patterns are analyzed annually to identify systemic gaps. Near-miss data informs training priorities. The clinic's incident response plan is tested via tabletop exercises with documented after-action reviews.

### Dimension 6: Access Management

This dimension measures whether PHI system access is granted appropriately, reviewed periodically, and revoked promptly on separation.

**Level 1:** Access is provisioned informally. No access inventory exists. Terminated staff may still have active credentials. Access is not reviewed after initial provisioning.

**Level 2:** Most terminated staff have their access revoked, but the process is informal and sometimes delayed. Access provisioning follows rough role guidelines but is not formally documented. No periodic access review occurs.

**Level 3:** Access is provisioned against a documented role matrix. Terminations trigger an access revocation checklist. Quarterly access reviews compare active grants against current workforce roster.

**Level 4:** Access reviews produce signed documentation. Excess access found during reviews is tracked to remediation with timestamps. The access review log shows what was found and what was done. Termination access revocation is tracked with same-day SLAs.

**Level 5:** Access provisioning is automated for common role patterns. Unusual access patterns (after-hours access, bulk downloads, access to unfamiliar patients) generate alerts. Access reviews are risk-tiered - higher-risk roles are reviewed more frequently.

## Moving From Level 2 to Level 3

The Level 2-to-3 transition is where most small clinics have the most room to improve. The shift is from "it mostly happens" to "it is documented and consistently done."

Steps by dimension:
- **Task ownership:** Write a compliance calendar. Name an owner for each task. Review it quarterly.
- **Evidence:** Create a filing structure before the next activity. Produce and file the artifact as part of completing the task.
- **Vendor/BAA:** Build a one-row-per-vendor spreadsheet today.
- **Training:** Convert your annual training to a logged event with a sign-in sheet and a standard agenda.
- **Incident handling:** Write a one-page incident intake form. Give every staff member the Privacy Officer's name.
- **Access management:** Complete one access review this quarter. Document what you found and what you changed.

None of these steps require new software. They require a practice administrator with four hours, a filing system, and follow-through.
