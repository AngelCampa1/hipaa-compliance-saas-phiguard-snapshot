The HIPAA Right of Access Initiative is one of OCR's most active enforcement programs. Most settlements stem from the same root cause: a clinic could not show a clear, dated record of when the request came in, what was sent, when it was sent, and what was charged. A simple log fixes that.

This template gives you the log structure, the fields OCR expects to see, and the rules for fees and denials so your front desk has one place to track every request.

## Why a written log is necessary

§ 164.524 gives patients the right to inspect and obtain a copy of their PHI in a designated record set. Thirty days to respond, one 60-day extension allowed with a written notice. If a complaint reaches OCR and you cannot prove timeliness, you have already lost the procedural argument. A log produced from the date of request is the cheapest evidence you can have.

## Required fields

At a minimum, the log should capture: request date, requester name and relationship to patient, patient identifier, records requested, format requested (paper, electronic, summary), delivery method, response date, staff handling the request, fee charged, denial reason if applicable, and date of any extension notice sent.

## Permissible fees

OCR's copying-fee guidance limits charges to actual labor for copying, supplies, postage, and preparation of an agreed-upon summary or explanation. You cannot charge for retrieval, for system maintenance, or for staff time spent reviewing the chart for release. Many state laws cap per-page rates lower than your actual cost, in which case the lower cap applies.

## Denials

Denials must be in writing and grounded in one of the limited reasons in § 164.524(a)(3): psychotherapy notes, information compiled in reasonably anticipated litigation, certain CLIA-related records before reporting, and the narrow grounds in § 164.524(a)(3)(i)-(iii). Reviewable denials require a process for the patient to request a licensed healthcare professional review.

## State-law overlays

State medical-records laws sometimes require faster turnaround (15 to 25 days is common) or different fee caps. The stricter rule wins. Your log should include a column noting which jurisdiction applies if you operate in more than one state.

## Enforcement gaps to audit

The patterns that produce settlements: requests routed to billing instead of the privacy officer, requests for an electronic copy answered with paper, third-party directives ignored, fees billed before delivery, and 30-day deadlines missed because the request sat in a fax tray. Audit your log monthly against these patterns.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the Patient Records Request Log as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes Required log fields: date, requester, records, format, response, fees, denial reason; 30-day response clock with 60-day extension notice template; Permissible fees aligned to OCR's copying-fee guidance; Limited denial reasons under § 164.524(a)(3) and the patient review right. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

PHIGuard positions this kind of artifact as part of day-to-day compliance operations: a task, an owner, evidence, and a repeatable review cadence. The goal is not to create more paperwork. The goal is to make the clinic able to show, with dated records, how it applied HIPAA requirements to the workflow in front of it.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
