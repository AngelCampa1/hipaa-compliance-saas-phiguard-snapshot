## Why a Physical Audit Matters

The HIPAA Security Rule treats the building as a control surface. Under 45 CFR § 164.310, a covered entity must implement policies and procedures that limit physical access to its electronic information systems and the facilities that house them, while ensuring that properly authorized access is allowed. Translated into clinic operations, that means a practice administrator should be able to walk every room and answer four questions: who can enter, who is watching, what gets locked, and what gets logged.

This checklist is built for practice administrators at small clinics who do not have a dedicated facilities or IT security team. It is organized room by room and category by category, with concrete items rather than abstract principles. Print it, walk the building with a clipboard, mark each item, and file the completed checklist with your Security Rule documentation. The Security Rule requires retention of policies and procedures for six years from the date of creation or last effective date (§ 164.530(j)(2)).

## Who Should Use It

Practice administrators, office managers, and HIPAA security officers at clinics with three to fifty staff. The checklist assumes a single-site practice with a reception area, exam rooms, a back office or business area, a server or network closet, paper records storage, and shared utility spaces. Multi-site practices should run the checklist independently for each location.

## How to Use It

Schedule a quarterly physical audit and a fresh audit any time the practice changes its lease, adds a new room, hires a new workforce member with key access, or terminates a workforce member. A finding is not a failure — finding gaps before OCR or a breach does is the entire point of the exercise. Track every flagged item to remediation with an owner and a due date.

## Regulatory Context

The physical safeguards section breaks into four required standards: facility access controls (§ 164.310(a)), workstation use (§ 164.310(b)), workstation security (§ 164.310(c)), and device and media controls (§ 164.310(d)). The PDF that accompanies this page lays out a checklist row for every implementation specification under those standards, plus the practical artifacts most small clinics miss — a current key inventory, a visitor log template, a disposal log, and a re-keying trigger list tied to your offboarding checklist.

## Common Findings at Small Clinics

The patterns that surface in physical audits are predictable. A reception workstation angled so the screen faces the waiting area. A back-office laptop logged in and unattended during the lunch hour. A box of charts marked for shredding sitting in an unsecured hallway for a week. Keys to the records room that were issued to a biller who left in 2023. A server closet that doubles as a supply closet so half the staff has the door code. None of these require a sophisticated attacker. They require a checklist, a quarterly walk-through, and a practice administrator who treats the audit as a recurring task rather than a one-time event.

The PDF below gives you that checklist. Pair it with your access control policy, your sanctions policy acknowledgment, and your offboarding checklist, and the physical layer of your compliance program becomes documented evidence rather than verbal assurance.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the Physical Security Audit checklist as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes Facility access controls — keys, badges, alarm codes, and after-hours entry logging; Workstation security — placement, screen privacy, automatic lock, and authorized use; Device and media controls — inventory, encryption, sanitization, and disposal records; Visitor management — sign-in, escort requirements, and restricted-area boundaries. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
