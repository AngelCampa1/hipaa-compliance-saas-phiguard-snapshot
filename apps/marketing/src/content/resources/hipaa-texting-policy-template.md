## Why a Messaging Policy Is Non-Negotiable

Texting is the default communication channel for most of your staff and most of your patients. That convenience runs directly into the transmission security requirement at 45 CFR § 164.312(e), which obligates a covered entity to implement technical safeguards to guard against unauthorized access to ePHI transmitted over an electronic communications network. Standard SMS, iMessage, WhatsApp, and personal email do not meet that standard. Secure messaging platforms with a signed Business Associate Agreement do.

Without a written policy, the clinic relies on each workforce member to make the right call in the moment a patient texts a question or a colleague asks for a quick update on a roomed patient. A policy removes the judgment call. It names the approved platforms, names the prohibited ones, defines the limited cases where SMS is acceptable, and specifies what may and may not be included in those messages.

## Who Should Use This Template

Practice administrators and HIPAA security officers at clinics that send appointment reminders by text, allow staff to use messaging apps for clinical coordination, or have not yet formalized which platform staff should use for what kind of communication. The template assumes a small-clinic context where the practice has a single approved secure messaging platform and a clearly defined boundary between that platform and consumer messaging apps.

## What the Policy Covers

The PDF includes a purpose and scope section tied to the transmission security standard, an approved platforms section that names the secure messaging vendor and confirms BAA coverage, a prohibited platforms section that lists consumer SMS, iMessage, WhatsApp, Signal, Telegram, personal email, and any other channel without a BAA, a patient communication section that distinguishes appointment reminders from clinical correspondence and specifies when written consent is required, an acceptable SMS content section that lists what may appear in an appointment reminder and what must never appear, a staff-to-staff messaging section that defines minimum-necessary expectations on the secure platform, a retention section that addresses when secure messages enter the designated record set, and an acknowledgment signature page.

## Adoption Steps

Choose your secure messaging vendor and confirm the BAA is signed before you publish the policy. Edit the bracketed fields in the template to name the vendor and any clinic-specific exceptions. Update your Notice of Privacy Practices to reflect SMS appointment reminders if you offer them. Capture patient consent at intake or on the patient portal. Train staff on the policy at a single staff meeting and collect signed acknowledgments. Re-issue the acknowledgment annually.

## Common Mistakes the Policy Prevents

The patterns that surface in messaging-related complaints are consistent. A staff member who texts a patient's lab result from a personal phone because the patient asked. A provider group chat in iMessage where colleagues discuss a roomed patient by initials. An appointment reminder that names the specialty so a family member who sees the screen learns something the patient had not disclosed. A former employee whose personal phone still contains months of clinical text threads. Each of these is an avoidable incident with a written policy and a single approved channel. The template gives you the policy. Your job is to enforce the channel.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the Texting Policy template as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes Approved messaging platforms — secure messaging vendors with a signed BAA; Prohibited platforms — consumer SMS, iMessage, WhatsApp, and personal email for PHI; Patient consent — when SMS communication requires documented consent and how to capture it; Acceptable SMS content — appointment reminders without diagnosis, test result, or specialty disclosure. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

PHIGuard positions this kind of artifact as part of day-to-day compliance operations: a task, an owner, evidence, and a repeatable review cadence. The goal is not to create more paperwork. The goal is to make the clinic able to show, with dated records, how it applied HIPAA requirements to the workflow in front of it.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
