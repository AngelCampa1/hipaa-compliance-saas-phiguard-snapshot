## Why this self-assessment exists

Most small clinics have some HIPAA work in place. A training log may live in one folder, BAAs in another, incident notes in email, and recurring reviews in a spreadsheet that only one person understands. That does not mean the program is ready for an OCR inquiry, payer audit, acquisition diligence request, or serious vendor incident.

This self-assessment answers a narrower question: can your clinic prove that the core HIPAA operating work is assigned, current, documented, and reviewable? It is not a legal opinion and it does not replace a formal risk analysis. It gives the practice administrator, privacy officer, security officer, or owner a structured way to find the weakest part of the clinic's HIPAA workflow before a real investigation exposes it.

## What the assessment scores

The worksheet uses five scoring areas. Each one maps to recurring work, not just policies that need to exist on paper.

| Area | What the score checks | What a low score usually means |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor and BAA coverage | Whether PHI-touching vendors are listed, classified, under BAA when required, and reviewed | The clinic may not know which vendors create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI |
| Risk analysis and remediation | Whether the clinic has a current risk analysis, risk register, owners, and target dates | The risk analysis may be stale, too generic, or disconnected from actual remediation work |
| Workforce training and access | Whether training, role access, sanctions, and offboarding are documented | The clinic may rely on memory instead of evidence when staff roles change |
| Incident readiness | Whether staff know how to report incidents and whether the clinic can document triage | Breach decisions may be handled in email without a complete record |
| Workflow sprawl | Whether PHI-adjacent work is scattered across inboxes, spreadsheets, chat, shared drives, and generic PM tools | The clinic may have compliance work, but no reliable source of truth |

The point is not to produce a flattering score. The useful result is a ranked list of weak spots.

## How to take it

Set aside 15 minutes with the person who actually knows how the clinic runs. In many small practices that is the office manager or practice administrator, not outside counsel. Answer based on current evidence, not policy intent.

Use this rule while scoring: if you cannot find the artifact in a few minutes, do not count it as present. A BAA that "should be somewhere" is not a signed BAA on file. A training session that people remember is not a dated training record. An incident plan nobody can locate is not operational readiness.

For each question, score the clinic as:

- **0 points:** no clear process or evidence exists.
- **1 point:** a process exists, but evidence is incomplete, stale, or scattered.
- **2 points:** the clinic has a documented process and current evidence.

After scoring, group the missed points by category. That grouping matters more than the total.

## How to interpret the score

**0 to 20: under-documented.** The clinic may be doing some HIPAA work, but it will struggle to prove it. Start with the basics: current vendor list, signed BAAs, named privacy/security responsibility, annual training records, and a current risk analysis.

**21 to 35: fragmented.** The clinic has real pieces in place, but the evidence is scattered. This is the most common small-clinic state. The next improvement is usually ownership and a single operating record, not another generic policy binder.

**36 to 45: operational but audit-fragile.** The core work exists, but the program may depend too heavily on one person. Focus on repeatable review dates, backup ownership, documented incident handling, and evidence retention.

**46 to 50: ready for deeper review.** A high score does not mean the clinic is done. It means the clinic has enough structure to move into a formal risk analysis update, vendor due diligence refresh, or internal audit without starting from scratch.

## Red flags the worksheet is designed to catch

A low score in a single area can be more important than the total score. Treat these answers as immediate follow-up items:

- No current list of vendors that touch PHI.
- BAAs stored in email with no status tracker.
- No dated risk analysis in the last 12 months or after a major operational change.
- Training completion tracked only by memory.
- Shared logins or role access that has not been reviewed after staff turnover.
- Incident reports handled through unstructured email threads.
- PHI-related tasks assigned in generic project tools without a clear BAA decision.
- Policies that name roles the clinic no longer has.

Each item should become a dated remediation task with an owner.

## What to do after the assessment

Pick the lowest-scoring category and turn it into a 30-day cleanup project. Do not try to fix the entire HIPAA program in one week.

If vendor coverage is weakest, build the vendor inventory first and classify each vendor as Business Associate, subcontractor relationship, conduit, no PHI, or needs review. If risk analysis is weakest, update the asset inventory and risk register before rewriting policies. If training is weakest, rebuild the roster and collect dated completion evidence. If incident readiness is weakest, run a tabletop exercise and document the decisions.

The assessment is useful because it forces the clinic to choose the next operational step. A clinic that knows its weakest area can improve faster than one that keeps adding disconnected templates.
