## The Requirement Most Clinics Know They Have and Have Not Met

45 CFR § 164.530(e) is a required implementation specification: covered entities must apply appropriate sanctions against workforce members who fail to comply with the privacy policies and procedures of the covered entity or the requirements of the Privacy Rule. The regulation further requires that these sanctions be documented. The documentation requirement at 45 CFR § 164.530(j) makes clear that sanctions records must be retained for 6 years.

In practice, most small clinics handle HIPAA violations informally. A conversation happens. A note may or may not be made. Six months later, no one can reconstruct what occurred, what was decided, or whether the same workforce member has violated the same policy before. This gap is not a minor documentation oversight — it is a pattern that OCR looks for when investigating a breach or conducting an audit. A covered entity that cannot demonstrate a consistent sanctions program is a covered entity that cannot demonstrate a functional HIPAA compliance program.

This template is a downloadable PDF that gives your Privacy Officer and management team everything needed to run a documented, consistent sanctions process from first report to resolution. It includes the log template itself, the severity rubric, a policy violation crosswalk, and a retention guide.

## What's in the PDF

### The Sanctions Log Template

The log is structured as a table. Each violation gets its own row. The columns are designed to capture all information needed to reconstruct the event and the response years later, without recording unnecessary PHI.

**Date of Incident.** The date the violation occurred, not the date it was discovered. Where the violation date is unknown, record "approximately [month/year]" and note uncertainty.

**Date Discovered.** The date the Privacy Officer or management became aware of the violation. The gap between incident and discovery date is important context for understanding the clinic's monitoring and reporting culture.

**Workforce Member Role.** The role or job title of the workforce member who committed the violation, not their name. The log is a compliance record, not an HR file. The HR personnel file should contain the name-specific documentation. The compliance log should be role-level to support pattern analysis (are violations clustering in a particular role or function?) without creating a PHI risk in the log itself.

**Description of Violation.** A plain-English description of what occurred: what the workforce member did or failed to do, what policy or regulation was implicated, and how the violation was discovered. The description should be specific enough that someone reading it six years later understands the event without needing additional context.

**Policy Violated.** The specific internal policy section and the corresponding regulatory citation. Example: "Access to PHI without business need — Clinic Policy §3.2 (Minimum Necessary Standard), 45 CFR § 164.502(b)."

**Sanctions Applied.** The specific sanction or sanctions applied, drawn from the five-level severity rubric. Be specific: "Written warning issued, additional training on minimum necessary standard required within 30 days" is a complete record. "Talked to employee" is not.

**Date Resolved.** The date the sanction was applied and any required corrective action (additional training, access modification) was completed. This is the date from which the 6-year retention clock runs for the specific entry.

**Documentation Reference.** A reference to any supporting documentation: the investigation notes, the written warning issued, the training completion record from remediation, the HR file reference if the matter was escalated. The log points to the documentation; it does not contain it.

### Sanctions Severity Rubric

The rubric maps violation categories to sanction levels. Five levels are defined:

**Level 1 — Verbal Warning.** Appropriate for: first-time, minor procedural violations (failing to use the cover sheet on a fax, leaving a workstation unlocked briefly). The verbal warning must be documented in the log. A verbal warning not in the log effectively did not happen for compliance purposes.

**Level 2 — Written Warning.** Appropriate for: repeated minor violations after a Level 1 warning; first-time violations of a more significant policy (sharing login credentials, accessing a record without business need, disclosing PHI in an unauthorized email). The written warning should be signed by the workforce member and placed in the HR file.

**Level 3 — Mandatory Additional Training.** This level is often applied alongside Level 1 or Level 2 rather than as a standalone sanction. It is the primary response when the violation appears to stem from knowledge gaps rather than disregard for policy. Training completion must be documented.

**Level 4 — Suspension of PHI Access.** Appropriate for: serious violations pending investigation (suspected intentional unauthorized access, suspected breach, suspected misuse), or for repeated violations at Level 2 that have not been corrected by training. Suspension of access is a technical action as well as a disciplinary one — the access revocation must be implemented in the EHR and other systems, not just noted on paper.

**Level 5 — Termination.** Appropriate for: intentional unauthorized access to PHI for personal benefit or curiosity; selling PHI; identity theft using patient information; any criminal conduct involving PHI; and egregious violations that cannot be remediated. Termination should be preceded by a documented investigation. Access revocation must occur on the same business day following termination.

### Policy Violation Crosswalk

The crosswalk maps the most common HIPAA violation types to their regulatory basis and the typical sanction range. Categories covered: unauthorized access to records, improper disclosure to third parties, failure to use minimum necessary, lost or stolen device with PHI, personal device with PHI without authorization, improper disposal of PHI, sharing access credentials, failure to report a known incident, and retaliatory disclosure.

The crosswalk supports consistent sanction decisions — the same violation category should result in the same sanction level regardless of who the workforce member is or who their manager is. Inconsistency in sanctions is both a compliance problem and an employment law liability.

### Escalation Triggers

Some violations require immediate escalation beyond the standard sanctions process:

- Suspected intentional unauthorized access for personal gain or curiosity
- Suspected sale of PHI or identity theft
- Any conduct suggesting criminal activity
- Any conduct by the Privacy Officer or Security Officer themselves (requires escalation to clinic ownership or governing board)
- Any violation involving a vendor or business associate (requires the BAA-defined notification process)

The escalation section identifies who must be notified, when, and what documentation must accompany the notification.

### Retention Guide

All documentation maintained to satisfy HIPAA requirements must be retained for 6 years from creation or last effective date per 45 CFR § 164.530(j). The retention guide explains:

- How to calculate the retention end date for each log entry
- What supporting documentation must be retained alongside the log entry (investigation notes, warning letters, training records from remediation)
- How to handle log entries for workforce members who have been terminated (retention obligation does not end when employment ends)
- When destruction of expired log entries is appropriate and how to document destruction

## Regulatory Basis

The sanctions requirement is at 45 CFR § 164.530(e)(1): "A covered entity must have and apply appropriate sanctions against members of its workforce who fail to comply with the privacy policies and procedures of the covered entity or the requirements of this subpart or subpart D of this part." Documentation of sanctions is required by 45 CFR § 164.530(j), with a 6-year retention period.

The parallel Security Rule sanctions requirement at 45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(C) requires covered entities to address sanctions for workforce members who violate security policies as part of the security management process.
