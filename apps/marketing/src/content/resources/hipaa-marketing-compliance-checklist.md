Most clinic marketing problems are not fancy. They come from three patterns: using a patient list to send promotional email without authorization, posting a testimonial or before/after photo without a written authorization, and dropping an analytics or ad pixel onto pages that handle PHI. Each one is a documented OCR investigation pathway.

This checklist walks through the rules that actually matter when a clinic markets itself, with citations to the regulations rather than vibes from a marketing blog.

## What HIPAA calls "marketing"

45 CFR 164.501 defines marketing as a communication about a product or service that encourages recipients to purchase or use it. Several communications are explicitly excluded: communications for treatment, case management or care coordination, and communications to describe a health-related product or service provided by the covered entity. The exclusion is narrower than people assume. The moment a third party pays you to promote their product, or you promote a product unrelated to a current treatment relationship, the communication is marketing and authorization is required.

## Authorization for marketing

45 CFR 164.508(a)(3) requires a valid HIPAA authorization for any marketing use or disclosure of PHI, with limited face-to-face exceptions. The authorization must contain the core elements in 45 CFR 164.508(c): description of information, named recipient, purpose, expiration, signature, and right to revoke. A verbal yes does not meet this standard. A social media DM does not meet this standard.

## Testimonials and before/after photos

A patient testimonial that identifies the patient or the clinical context is a use of PHI for marketing. Get a signed authorization. The same applies to before/after photos in dermatology, dental, plastic surgery, weight loss, and similar specialties — even when the face is cropped, if the image plus context could identify the person, it is PHI. Apply 45 CFR 164.514(a)-(b) de-identification standards before publishing without authorization, and document how you met them.

## Email marketing and patient lists

Do not pull a marketing email list out of the EHR or scheduling system without authorization. The treatment exception covers reminders for the patient's current care. It does not cover newsletters about new services, seasonal promotions, or product lines. Maintain a separate marketing list built from explicit opt-in.

## Website tracking pixels and analytics

HHS December 2022 guidance, updated in March 2024, addresses online tracking technologies on covered entity websites and apps. The current OCR position: tracking technologies on user-authenticated pages and on pages that address specific health conditions or providers can disclose PHI to the tracking vendor, requiring a BAA or removal. Standard practice: no third-party tracking on patient portal pages, appointment scheduling pages, or condition-specific landing pages without a BAA in place.

## Social media

Never confirm someone is a patient. Do not respond to a public review with anything that acknowledges the relationship. Do not post photos taken inside clinical areas. Train staff that liking, sharing, or quoting a patient post can constitute disclosure.

A clinic that follows this checklist can run paid ads, publish blog content, send newsletters, and grow without quietly walking into an OCR file.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the HIPAA-Compliant Marketing Checklist for Clinics as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes When a communication crosses HIPAA's marketing definition under 45 CFR 164.501; Authorization requirements for marketing uses of PHI per 45 CFR 164.508(a)(3); Patient testimonial and before/after photo authorization rules; De-identification standards under 45 CFR 164.514(a)-(b) for marketing content. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

PHIGuard positions this kind of artifact as part of day-to-day compliance operations: a task, an owner, evidence, and a repeatable review cadence. The goal is not to create more paperwork. The goal is to make the clinic able to show, with dated records, how it applied HIPAA requirements to the workflow in front of it.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
