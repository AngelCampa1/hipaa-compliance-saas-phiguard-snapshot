Plenty of practices invest in compliance programs without first confirming HIPAA actually applies to them. Others assume it does not apply and discover the opposite after a complaint. This guide gives you a clear path to the right answer.

## The three categories

Under § 160.103, a covered entity is one of three things: a covered healthcare provider, a health plan, or a healthcare clearinghouse. Most clinics fall into the first category if they meet the electronic transaction trigger. Health plans cover insurers, HMOs, employer group health plans of a certain size, and government programs. Clearinghouses translate transactions between providers and payers.

## The transaction trigger for providers

A healthcare provider becomes a covered entity by transmitting health information in electronic form in connection with a transaction for which HHS has adopted a standard. Those transactions are listed in 45 CFR Part 162 and include claims, eligibility inquiries, prior authorizations, claim status, enrollment, premium payments, referrals, remittance advice, and coordination of benefits.

The trigger is electronic transmission, not the existence of electronic records. A practice that keeps an EHR but submits all claims on paper has technically not crossed the threshold. A practice that uses a billing service to submit claims electronically has crossed it through the agent.

## Examples that ARE covered

A primary care clinic that submits claims through a clearinghouse. A dental office that runs eligibility checks through a payer portal. A solo psychiatrist whose biller files Medicare claims electronically. A physical therapy practice that receives electronic remittance advice.

## Examples that are NOT covered

A concierge cash-only practice that submits no claims, runs no electronic eligibility checks, and uses no clearinghouse. A life insurer underwriting policies (life insurance is not a covered health plan). A small employer's self-funded plan below the size threshold that does not perform standard transactions electronically.

## Hybrid entity considerations

A university with both a teaching hospital and unrelated academic departments may designate itself a hybrid entity under § 164.105, applying HIPAA only to the healthcare components. The designation requires written documentation and operational separation. Mid-size clinics rarely need this; multi-purpose organizations sometimes do.

## Business associate vs covered entity

If you handle PHI but only on behalf of a covered entity (a billing company, an IT vendor, a transcription service), you are a business associate, not a covered entity. Your HIPAA obligations flow through the BAA you sign and through direct application of the Security Rule and parts of the Privacy Rule under HITECH.

## What to do once covered

Designate a Privacy Officer and Security Officer. Complete a § 164.308(a)(1) risk analysis. Inventory your business associates and execute BAAs. Adopt written policies. Train your workforce. Build the audit trail you will need if OCR ever asks how you got there.

## How to use this resource in a live HIPAA program

Use the Covered Entity Determination Guide as a working control record, not as a document that gets filed once and forgotten. Start by naming the owner, the affected workflow, the systems or vendors involved, and the date the review was performed. Then walk through each line with the staff who actually handle the work. HIPAA documentation is strongest when it reflects real operations: who touches PHI, where ePHI is created or transmitted, what access is necessary for the role, and what evidence proves the safeguard is operating.

For this resource, the practical evidence usually includes The three covered entity categories under § 160.103; The electronic-transaction trigger that pulls providers into HIPAA; Side-by-side examples of covered and non-covered practices; Hybrid entity considerations for mixed-purpose organizations. Keep those items with screenshots, vendor records, policy acknowledgements, training logs, or access-review notes when they support the answer. If a line cannot be completed, record the exception, assign an owner, and set a due date instead of leaving the item blank.

## Evidence to keep with the completed resource

Save the final version with the review date, reviewer name, and any follow-up tasks. If the resource supports an administrative safeguard, connect it to your risk analysis and policy review history. If it supports a technical or physical safeguard, keep the configuration evidence or walk-through notes that show what was checked. If it supports a patient-rights or disclosure workflow, keep the request log, response dates, and any correspondence that explains the decision.

PHIGuard positions this kind of artifact as part of day-to-day compliance operations: a task, an owner, evidence, and a repeatable review cadence. The goal is not to create more paperwork. The goal is to make the clinic able to show, with dated records, how it applied HIPAA requirements to the workflow in front of it.

## Review cadence

Review this resource at least once a year and sooner after a material change. Material changes include a new EHR, billing platform, AI tool, telehealth workflow, location, vendor, role, state-law overlay, or incident pattern. During the review, confirm that the source policy still matches current operations, that the listed owner still has authority to make changes, and that unresolved exceptions have not aged into accepted risk without leadership approval.
