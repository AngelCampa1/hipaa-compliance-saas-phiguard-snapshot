## When a Breach Has Occurred — What the Law Requires You to Send

A breach of unsecured PHI triggers notification obligations that are specific, time-bound, and content-prescribed by the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule. 45 CFR § 164.404(c) enumerates exactly what a breach notification letter must contain. A notification that omits any required element, or that contains inaccurate information, can be cited by OCR as a violation of the notification requirements independent of the breach itself.

The notification letter is often the first communication between a covered entity and an affected patient after a breach. It sets the patient's expectations, establishes the covered entity's credibility, and begins the relationship with HHS. Getting it right matters — not only for compliance, but for the practice's relationship with its patients.

This downloadable PDF gives your Privacy Officer a ready-to-use template pre-structured around the five required content elements. It includes guidance notes on what each section must say, what it must not say, and how to handle the situations where the template requires customization. It also includes the notification path checklist that maps your obligations based on the number of affected individuals.

## What's in the PDF

### The Letter Template

The template is structured around the five required content elements from 45 CFR § 164.404(c). Each section is pre-drafted with placeholder language and guidance notes in brackets.

**Required Element 1 — A brief description of what happened, including the date of the breach and the date of discovery of the breach, if known.**

The description should be factual, specific, and complete. It should identify: what type of incident occurred (unauthorized access, theft, misdirected communication, etc.), when it occurred or approximately when it occurred, when the covered entity discovered it, and whether the breach involved electronic or paper records. The guidance notes explain how to handle the common situation where the exact breach date is unknown — the letter should state the approximate date range and note that the exact date is under investigation if the investigation is ongoing.

**Required Element 2 — A brief description of the types of unsecured protected health information that were involved in the breach.**

This element requires identifying the categories of PHI involved: name, address, date of birth, Social Security number, diagnosis codes, treatment information, insurance information, financial account information, and so on. The letter must describe the types of PHI but must not contain additional PHI about the individual. The guidance notes explain how to handle breaches involving sensitive categories — behavioral health information, HIV status, substance use disorder records — where the description of PHI types is itself sensitive.

**Required Element 3 — Any steps individuals should take to protect themselves from potential harm resulting from the breach.**

This section is tailored to the type of PHI involved. If financial account numbers were exposed, the steps include placing a fraud alert and monitoring credit. If Social Security numbers were exposed, the steps include placing a credit freeze and considering identity theft protection services. If the breach involved only health information without financial identifiers, the steps focus on monitoring for identity theft using health information (medical identity theft). The PDF includes pre-drafted language for the most common PHI categories.

**Required Element 4 — A brief description of what the covered entity involved is doing to investigate the breach, to mitigate harm to individuals, and to protect against any further such breaches.**

This section describes the covered entity's response actions. It should be specific and honest. Vague language ("we are taking steps to prevent future incidents") is less credible and less protective than specific language about what the clinic has actually done or is doing. The guidance notes explain how to describe actions appropriately, avoid creating additional liability by overpromising, and what to say when the investigation is still ongoing at the time the letter is sent.

**Required Element 5 — Contact procedures for individuals to ask questions or learn additional information, which shall include a toll-free telephone number, an email address, website, or postal address.**

The contact information should be staffed and responsive. If the clinic is using a dedicated breach response email address or phone line, specify it. The guidance notes address the common situation where a small clinic does not have the capacity to dedicate a phone line — options and their tradeoffs are explained.

### Notification Path Checklist

The checklist maps your notification obligations based on the number of affected individuals and their location.

**Path A — Fewer than 500 individuals.** Individual notification by first-class mail to last known address. HHS notification is required but can be delayed until the end of the calendar year in which the breach occurred, using the HHS online portal. Notification must occur no later than 60 days after the end of that calendar year. No media notification is required.

**Path B — 500 or more individuals, not concentrated in a single state.** Individual notification within 60 days of discovery. HHS notification within 60 days of discovery via the HHS Breach Notification Portal (the submission will appear on the HHS "Wall of Shame" public breach database). No media notification required unless 500 or more individuals reside in a single state or jurisdiction.

**Path C — 500 or more residents of a single state or jurisdiction.** Same as Path B, plus media notification under 45 CFR § 164.406. Media notification must be provided to prominent media outlets serving the state or jurisdiction within 60 days of discovery, via a press release or equivalent. The checklist identifies how to determine which media outlets qualify as "prominent" for your state.

**Path D — Insufficient contact information.** When the covered entity does not have sufficient contact information for 10 or more affected individuals, substitute notice is required: a conspicuous posting on the home page of the covered entity's website for 90 days and a toll-free phone number active for 90 days, or major media outlets in the geographic areas where the affected individuals live.

### HHS Portal Submission Guide

The HHS online breach reporting portal (at hhs.gov/breach) requires specific information about the breach, the affected individuals, and the covered entity's response. The submission guide walks through the portal's required fields, explains how to characterize the breach type and safeguards that were in place, and notes what information HHS will use to assess whether additional investigation is warranted.

## Regulatory Basis

The HIPAA Breach Notification Rule at Subpart D of Part 164 (45 CFR §§ 164.400–164.414) governs breach notification obligations for covered entities and business associates. The required content of individual notification is specified at 45 CFR § 164.404(c). The media notification requirement is at 45 CFR § 164.406. The HHS notification requirement is at 45 CFR § 164.408.

The 60-day notification deadline is measured from the date the covered entity discovers the breach — or, for a business associate breach, from the date the business associate notifies the covered entity. The 60-day period is a maximum; notifications should be sent as soon as reasonably possible once the investigation supports doing so.
