## Why State Law Overlay Matters

HIPAA is a federal minimum standard. States pass privacy laws that are more protective of patients than HIPAA, and when they do, covered entities operating in those states must comply with the stricter state requirement, not just the federal floor.

For small clinics with a single physical location, the relevant state law is the state they operate in. For telehealth providers serving patients across state lines, multiple state laws may apply simultaneously, one for each state where a patient is located.

This matrix covers the three state laws most commonly encountered in multi-state practice and telehealth contexts. Two caveats before using it:

**This matrix is a general reference, not legal advice.** State laws change. Enforcement interpretations evolve. Verify current requirements with qualified legal counsel before making compliance decisions based on any specific state overlay.

**HIPAA preemption analysis is complex.** When HIPAA and state law appear to conflict, a formal preemption analysis is required. This matrix highlights areas where state law is stricter than HIPAA. It does not substitute for preemption analysis.

## The Matrix: Federal HIPAA vs Three State Overlays

### Dimension 1: Training Requirements

| Requirement | Federal HIPAA | California CMIA | Texas HB 300 | New York SHIELD Act |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Training required | Yes — periodic training for all workforce members (45 CFR §164.308(a)(5)) | Yes — required for employees who handle medical information | **Yes — more specific than HIPAA:** training on state law and HIPAA required; employees who receive PHI without authorization must be trained | Yes — for employees who access private information |
| Frequency | Periodic (not specified) | Not specified | Periodic; required when policies change | Reasonable frequency |
| Documentation | Must be maintained (retention required) | Not specifically required by statute | Training records maintained | Reasonable practice |
| **Stricter than HIPAA?** | Baseline | Generally comparable | **Yes** — explicit state law training content required | Generally comparable |

**Texas HB 300 action item:** Training programs for Texas-applicable practices must cover the requirements of HB 300 specifically — not just HIPAA. Training that covers only federal HIPAA requirements is insufficient for Texas compliance. If your EHR vendor or training platform provides standard HIPAA training, verify that it addresses HB 300's requirements.

### Dimension 2: Breach Notification

| Requirement | Federal HIPAA | California CMIA | Texas HB 300 | New York SHIELD Act |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Notification to individuals | Within 60 days of discovery (45 CFR §164.404) | "In the most expedient time possible" and without unreasonable delay | Without unreasonable delay; no specific number of days | "In the most expedient time possible" |
| Notification to state AG | Not required under HIPAA | Required if breach affects 500+ California residents | Not separately required by HB 300; reporting follows HIPAA | Required for breaches affecting New York residents |
| Breach threshold | Impermissible disclosure of unsecured PHI (4-factor risk assessment) | Unauthorized acquisition of medical information | Unauthorized acquisition of sensitive personal information; HB 300 follows HIPAA breach standard for covered entities | Unauthorized acquisition of private information (broader than HIPAA's PHI) |
| Substitute notice | Available for large-scale breaches (website notice, media) | Available with conditions | Follows HIPAA | Available with conditions |
| **Stricter than HIPAA?** | Baseline | **Potentially** — "most expedient" may be stricter than 60 days; AG notification for large breaches | Generally follows HIPAA for covered entities | **Potentially** — applies to broader data types than PHI; AG notification |

**California action item:** The CMIA's "most expedient time possible" standard is potentially stricter than HIPAA's 60-day outer limit. California-facing practices should plan for faster breach notification timelines than the federal 60-day maximum and should contact the California AG if a breach affects 500 or more California residents.

**New York action item:** The SHIELD Act covers "private information" more broadly than HIPAA's PHI definition — including Social Security numbers, financial account information, biometric data, and login credentials. A security incident that doesn't meet HIPAA's breach threshold may still trigger SHIELD Act notification if private information was affected. The SHIELD Act also requires notifying the NY AG for breaches affecting New York residents.

### Dimension 3: Patient Access Rights

| Requirement | Federal HIPAA | California CMIA | Texas HB 300 | New York SHIELD Act |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Right to access records | Yes — within 30 days (extendable to 60) (45 CFR §164.524) | Yes — inspection within 5 working days, copies within 15 days (Health & Safety Code §123110) | Follows HIPAA | N/A (SHIELD Act is primarily a data security law, not a patient rights law) |
| Access fees | Limited — covered entities may charge a reasonable fee | Limited fees permitted | Follows HIPAA generally | N/A |
| Electronic format | Required when patient requests electronic format | Required | Follows HIPAA | N/A |
| Right to restrict disclosures | Limited right — covered entities may agree but are not required to accept restrictions | More expansive restriction rights under CMIA | Follows HIPAA | N/A |
| **Stricter than HIPAA?** | Baseline | **Yes** — 5 working days for inspection (Health & Safety Code §123110) is stricter than HIPAA's 30 days | Generally follows HIPAA | N/A |

**California action item:** California Health & Safety Code §123110 entitles adult patients to inspect their records within 5 working days of a written request and to receive copies within 15 days — stricter than HIPAA's 30-day standard. California practices should have a triage process for patient records requests to identify and route them within the §123110 window.

### Dimension 4: Records Retention

| Requirement | Federal HIPAA | California CMIA / California Health & Safety Code | Texas law | New York law |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum retention period | 6 years for HIPAA documentation (policies, training records, BAAs, etc.); medical records retention governed by state law | Adults: 7 years from last date of service; minors: 7 years from last date of service or until the patient turns 19, whichever is later | Adults: 10 years from last treatment; minors: until age 21 or 10 years, whichever is longer | Adults: 6 years; minors: until age 21 |
| Medical records specifically | Not directly governed by HIPAA — state law controls | 7 years (minimum) | **10 years** — stricter than most states | 6 years |
| **Stricter than HIPAA?** | HIPAA governs compliance documentation, not medical records | Stricter than HIPAA's 6-year documentation standard for medical records | **Yes — 10-year medical record retention** | Comparable |

**Texas action item:** Texas requires 10-year retention of adult patient medical records, compared to the 6-year minimum in HIPAA's documentation retention rule. Texas practices must configure EHR and records management systems for a 10-year retention standard.

## Multi-State Telehealth Considerations

When a clinic offers telehealth services to patients in multiple states, the compliance picture changes in four ways:

**Which state's law applies:** The state where the patient is located at the time of service governs. A California-based patient receiving telehealth from a Texas clinic may have California CMIA protections. Each state where you actively serve patients requires a jurisdictional analysis.

**Breach notification:** A breach affecting patients in multiple states may trigger notification obligations in each state, not just the state where the clinic is located. The strictest applicable notification timeline governs.

**Training:** Training that covers California's CMIA requirements must also address Texas HB 300's requirements if the clinic serves Texas patients. State-specific training modules may be necessary.

**Practical approach for small telehealth practices:** Identify the states in which you actively serve patients. Conduct a state-law privacy review for each. Applying the most stringent applicable requirement as your operating standard is often more efficient than maintaining separate state-specific policies for each jurisdiction.

## Using This Matrix

This matrix identifies where state law may impose stricter requirements than federal HIPAA. It is not a substitute for:
- Legal counsel review of your specific state obligations
- A formal HIPAA preemption analysis where state and federal law appear to conflict
- Verification of current state law text — state laws are amended periodically

Treat this matrix as a starting point for conversations with your legal counsel, not a final compliance determination.
