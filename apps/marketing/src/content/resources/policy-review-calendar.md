## Why Policies Need a Review Calendar

HIPAA's documentation requirement (45 CFR §164.530(i)(2)(ii)) requires covered entities to periodically review and update privacy and security documentation in response to environmental or operational changes. Policies written three years ago and never reviewed are not compliant documentation. They are historical artifacts that may no longer reflect the clinic's actual practices.

Policies also become obsolete without anyone noticing. A security policy's section on "authorized devices" written when the clinic had three desktop computers and no staff smartphones no longer applies when the clinic has 15 devices including phones, tablets, and a telehealth laptop. The policy states a standard that no longer matches practice, which is the opposite of what a policy is for.

A review calendar prevents both problems. It schedules policy reviews as annual events with named owners and attestation deadlines. "We'll review policies when something changes" is not a sufficient operating principle — things change gradually, and the gap only surfaces when an investigation makes it visible.

## How the Review Calendar Works

Each row in the calendar represents one policy, or a group of related policies small enough to review together in one session. Each row has:

- **Review month:** The month the review is scheduled in the annual cycle
- **Policy name:** The specific policy being reviewed
- **Owner:** The person responsible for leading the review — reading the policy, comparing it to current practice, making updates, and obtaining attestation
- **Attestation deadline:** The last day of the review month, by which the review must be completed and documented
- **Last review date:** The date this policy was last reviewed and updated (even if the outcome is "no changes needed" — the review itself is the event)
- **Notes:** Any significant changes made, or a "no changes required" notation

## Policies Most Clinics Skip

These are the policies most often absent, outdated, or not in a reviewable form at small practices:

**Sanction Policy:** Required by §164.308(a)(1)(ii)(C). Most small clinics have a general reference to sanctions in their employee handbook but no dedicated HIPAA sanction policy specifying the range of sanctions and the review process. This gap is frequently cited in OCR investigations.

**Automatic Logoff Policy:** Required by §164.312(a)(2)(iii). Many clinics have a system configuration that enforces screen locks but no written policy documenting the standard (e.g., screen locks after 15 minutes). The policy documents the decision; the system configuration implements it.

**Unique User Identification Policy:** Required by §164.312(a)(2)(i). Shared accounts violate this requirement. The policy prohibiting shared accounts must be in writing, reviewed annually, and matched by actual system configuration.

**BYOD Policy:** Not explicitly required by name, but device controls under §164.310(d) and transmission security under §164.312(e) together require that PHI on personal devices is addressed. Most clinics allow personal device use informally and have never written down the rules.

**BAA Management Policy:** Not a separately named requirement under HIPAA, but the BAA requirements throughout §164.308(b) and §164.504(e) together create the policy need. How does your clinic identify when a BAA is needed How does it track execution and renewal The written procedure for this is the BAA management policy.

## Attestation Records That Hold Up

A policy review is documented by two things:
1. A notation in the policy document itself ("Last reviewed: [date], no changes required" OR "Version [X], effective [date], [summary of changes]")
2. A signed attestation from the policy owner confirming the review was completed

The attestation should be a dated, signed document, not an email thread. A two-line attestation ("I reviewed this policy on [date] and confirm it is current and accurate as of that date. [Signature] [Date]") is sufficient.

Attestation records are retained per the 6-year documentation retention requirement. When OCR asks "how do you know your policies are current?", the attestation records are the answer.
